\subsection{Deployment Readiness}
\label{ssec:deployment}
% The organizing payoff: score each family on the 3 deployment-readiness criteria
% (perf, fidelity, threat-fit). Qualitative now; per-scheme specifics \needswork.
The performance numbers above are one of three criteria that together determine whether
a scheme is likely to be \emph{deployed}: \cref{sec:intro}'s \emph{performance},
\emph{fidelity} (impact on output quality), and \emph{threat-model fit} (an adversary
model appropriate to the deployment, not maximal). \cref{tab:deployment} scores the
families on all three. The synthesis is deliberately not a ranking of families by
security: it shows that deployment readiness comes from \emph{balancing} the three axes,
not maximizing one. This family-level profile is the complement of the per-use-case
comparison (\cref{tab:performance}): the profiles say what each family is \emph{good at},
while the use-case view says which family is the deployable choice \emph{for a given
stage}---together they answer ``what should I deploy for my use case?''

\subsection{Graph-RAG as a Retrieval Setting}
% Woven treatment (decision: short dedicated subsection, not a parallel track).
% Graph-RAG (LightRAG, Microsoft GraphRAG) indexes a corpus as a knowledge graph;
% privacy work here is sparse (Ning et al. KG-QA privacy; Compass for encrypted
% search; Opal's in-enclave KG traversal). Bodea explicitly excludes this setting.
Graph-RAG systems such as LightRAG~\citep{guo_lightrag} index a corpus as a knowledge
graph; their retrieval surface is entity/relation ANN plus \emph{one-hop} neighborhood
expansion and a source-chunk fan-out---not multi-hop reasoning or GNN message-passing.
That shape determines which mechanisms apply: ANN matching is served by
Compass/Pacmann~\citep{zhu_compass,pacmann}, the distinctive adjacency/degree leak by
volume-hiding multi-maps (XorMM/FLASH)~\citep{xormm,flash_emm}, traversal-integrity by
ZKGraph~\citep{zkgraph}, and output-extraction (AGEA-class) by anonymization or
output-DP~\citep{ning_arog,tan_privgemo,tang_dprag}. Conversely, secure GNN-inference
and graph-analytics schemes are off-target for this setting and we cite them only as
lineage. Bodea et al.~\citep{bodea_sok} exclude graph-RAG entirely; the near-empty
graph rows of \cref{tab:gapmap} are the resulting open frontier.

\subsection{Finding: the Field-Wide Side-Channel Blind Spot}
\label{ssec:sidechannel}

Reading down the residual-leakage axis of the taxonomy (\cref{tab:threat-taxonomy})
yields a uniform answer: \emph{every} scheme places microarchitectural, cache, timing,
power, and physical channels out of scope. GELO excludes ``side channels
originating inside the TEE \dots\ and accelerator-side leakage beyond raw memory
reads'' \citep{belikov_gelo}; Opal devotes a paragraph to enumerating the
in-enclave side channels it does \emph{not} defend \citep{kaviani_opal}; the
remainder simply omit them. This is a collective blind spot worth naming,
because it falls hardest on the schemes least able to absorb it: the TEE and
hybrid designs anchor their entire trust in hardware whose best-documented
weakness is exactly microarchitectural and transient-execution leakage---the
Spectre/Foreshadow-class attacks \needswork{cite Spectre, Foreshadow, PLATYPUS,
and the TEE side-channel surveys}. A scheme that trusts a TEE while declaring
side channels out of scope is assuming away the dominant historical failure
mode of that very anchor.

We do not close this gap: side channels are out of scope for this SoK as
well, and current confidential-computing hardware
(TDX-backed CVMs, GPUs in confidential mode) materially raises the bar against
them \citep{kaviani_opal}. But we record it as a systematisation finding and a
standing item for future work: the field has converged on a threat model that
structurally excludes the attack class most likely to break its dominant trust
anchor.
