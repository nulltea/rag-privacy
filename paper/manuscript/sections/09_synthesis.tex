\section{Synthesis}
\label{sec:synthesis}

This section draws the per-family results of \cref{sec:crypto}--\cref{sec:retrieval} together
into three views: the three-way trade-off that locates each family
(\cref{ssec:design-space}), per-use-case deployment recommendations (\cref{ssec:deployment}),
and the cross-cutting findings and open problems that no single family section could state on
its own (\cref{ssec:findings}). The central finding is that no family dominates:
which scheme is deployable depends on the application, on the constraint it can least afford to relax.

\subsection{Design Space}
\label{ssec:design-space}

Every family maximizes at most two of the three criteria and pays on the
third, and the security basis axis of \cref{sec:threat-axes} explains why the third differs so
much. Cryptographic inference
(\cref{sec:crypto}) attains exact fidelity and a provable security basis, and pays one to three
orders of magnitude in performance. A GPU-TEE (\cref{sec:tee}) matches that exact fidelity at
near-plaintext speed, but its basis is the silicon: the guarantee reduces to hardware whose side
channels every threat model excludes, and to the availability of the accelerator that holds it.
Static obfuscation (\cref{sec:obfuscation}) is the cheapest, running at near-plaintext speed throughout, but
rests on a heuristic basis and loses fidelity in proportion to how aggressively it hides. Differential privacy keeps
that near-plaintext cost and adds a formal but probabilistic guarantee, paying again in fidelity
through the budget $\varepsilon$ (\cref{sec:dp}). The hybrid split (\cref{sec:hybrid}) recovers
exact fidelity and a basis stronger than static obfuscation on commodity hardware, and pays
instead in the TEE$\leftrightarrow$GPU serialization that its offloading induces. The criterion a
family gives up is forced by the basis it rests on, not incidental, so no single point on this
surface dominates the rest. Which trade-off a deployment can accept is what selects the scheme: a
real-time service is bound by its latency budget, a regulated workload by the security basis it
must be able to prove, a cost-sensitive one by the hardware it already has, and a
quality-critical one by the accuracy loss it can tolerate. The next section reads the surface through
these constraints, use case by use case.

The same trade-off applies differently in each use case, because the constraint a deployment can
least afford to relax changes with the task. We give the recommendation use case by use case.

\emph{Generation} is dominated by the cost of autoregressive decoding, so its binding
constraint is latency. A confidential accelerator (\cref{sec:tee}) can be most attractive option for
an operator that has access to one, running the full model at near-plaintext speed, but the hardware is
scarce and few deployments own it. Where it is absent, static obfuscation leaves the
architecture intact and runs on commodity GPUs with stock inference software at near-plaintext
cost, at the price of a heuristic basis. The hybrid
split is a weaker fit here than its commodity-hardware advantage suggests, because its
TEE$\leftrightarrow$GPU round-trips, one per non-linear step and so multiplied across layers and
generated tokens, dominate its decoding latency.
MPC and FHE pay one to three orders of magnitude, which is hard to justify for single-user
outsourcing; MPC's natural setting is instead \emph{collaborative} inference among mutually
distrusting data holders, such as joint analysis over disjoint institutional records, where no
party can be trusted to aggregate and a cryptographic guarantee across parties is essential.

\emph{Embedding and reranking} run models far smaller than a generative decoder, which changes the
trade-off: the workload does not need a high-end accelerator, so paying for a confidential GPU
is unnecessary. This is the hybrid split's natural setting. It keeps a small trusted core in a
commodity CPU-TEE and offloads to untrusted commodity GPUs (\cref{sec:hybrid}), giving exact
output without enterprise hardware, and the short, non-autoregressive workload makes its
round-trip cost far less acute than in generation. Static obfuscation and local DP run at
near-plaintext speed where a heuristic or probabilistic basis is acceptable, and a
cryptographic encoder (SHAFT, Euston) is the choice only when an exact and provable guarantee
is required.

\emph{Dense retrieval} divides by what must be hidden. When the access pattern and the
inter-document structure must stay hidden, the access-hiding schemes are the appropriate choice: private
information retrieval (Panther, PIR-RAG) returns a record without revealing which one, and
homomorphic scoring (RemoteRAG, TRSE) keeps the similarity values encrypted. When near-plaintext
speed is the priority and a bounded, quantified comparability leak is tolerable, CAPRISE is the
preferable option. Its conditional distance-comparison-preserving encryption hides the embeddings at
roughly $1\times$ cost, its query-side differential privacy blocks embedding inversion, and
unlike SAP it conceals the inter-document structure (\cref{ssec:retr-dense}). The deciding
question is whether that residual leak is acceptable for the corpus.

\emph{Graph retrieval} splits by whether a cryptographic guarantee is required. Its only
option at near-plaintext speed is heuristic anonymization (PrivGemo, ARoG), which pseudonymizes entities
and prunes structure at no cryptographic cost, but is lossy and carries no provable guarantee. A
scheme with a cryptographic guarantee instead pays overhead, and there the choice trades cost
against which structural channel it closes (\cref{ssec:retr-graph}). Hiding which node a query
touches requires client-side cryptography (batched PIR in Pacmann, an ORAM-wrapped index in
Compass) or the hardware route of a TEE (Opal); the cryptographic route shifts cost and state
onto the client, the TEE route onto trusted hardware. Hiding the adjacency volume requires a
volume-hiding encrypted multi-map (XorMM, FLASH) at the cost of storage overhead. No single
scheme closes all three channels simultaneously.

\begin{openproblem}
\label{op:no-e2e}
\emph{No confidential pipeline from query to answer.} Confidential retrieval is well covered:
RemoteRAG protects the query and the scoring end to end (\cref{ssec:retr-dense}), and confidential
generation exists separately in the inference families (\cref{sec:crypto}--\cref{sec:hybrid}). No
single design joins them, so a pipeline that stays confidential from the query through to the
generated answer must compose a private-retrieval scheme with a private-generation scheme across
the retrieval-generation boundary. Whether such a composition preserves both per-stage guarantees,
and at what combined fidelity cost, is uncharacterized (\cref{op:dp-compose}); the prior
RAG-privacy SoK reaches the same gap~\citep{bodea_sok}. \emph{Which retrieval-generation
compositions preserve their guarantees, and at what combined fidelity cost?}
\end{openproblem}

\subsection{Cross-Cutting Findings}
\label{ssec:findings}

\paragraph{Client reliance, offline setup, and multi-tenancy.}
The trust-anchor axis of \cref{sec:threat} brackets the client by construction, since a client
need not be named as its own trusted base, hiding how much work several schemes shift onto it.
Cryptographic schemes make the user a compute party in 2PC or run PIR and ORAM retrieval from the
client (\cref{sec:crypto},~\cref{sec:retrieval}); covariant weight obfuscation (AloePri, KV-Cloak,
ObfusLM) has the client re-key the model and hold the secret, so the checkpoint is per-client and
one model cannot serve many tenants (\cref{sec:obfuscation}); and local DP runs the model up to
the noise-injection point on the client and draws the noise there, DP-Forward trading more client
compute for more utility as that point moves deeper (\cref{sec:dp},~\citealp{du_dpforward}). The
hybrid split mostly avoids this by hiding dynamically, except SCX, which keeps the client encoding
each token and recovering the first and last blocks while the cloud computes the rest
(\cref{sec:hybrid}). Retrieval adds its own load: the client embeds and perturbs the query,
Compass holds an ORAM position map and runs a multi-round controller, and Pacmann preprocesses the
database (\cref{sec:retrieval}). Two reasons account for this: structurally, local DP and
covariant weight obfuscation can originate the secret only on the client, forgoing multi-tenancy;
for performance, a client-side cache, compute party, or embedder lowers server cost or saves
rounds. The TEE is the workaround, Opal hiding the same access pattern with only a key and a
counter on the client and so trading the client's burden for trust in the hardware
(\cref{sec:retrieval}).

\paragraph{Cryptographic and hybrid schemes force a custom serving path.}
Whether a scheme runs on an off-the-shelf inference server is a deployment cost the mechanism
tables omit. A custom serving path is required whenever the secret is applied during inference,
in one of two degrees. Cryptographic inference is the heavier: it replaces the model's operators,
evaluating the non-linearities through secret sharing, oblivious transfer, or homomorphic
arithmetic, so the server runs a secure-computation protocol in place of the model
(\cref{sec:crypto}). The hybrid split and KV-Cloak are lighter: the model's own compute still
runs on the GPU, but a refreshable transform is interposed on the activations or the KV-cache and
must be injected and unwound each request, so the serving path carries operators the stock stack
lacks (\cref{sec:hybrid},~\cref{sec:obfuscation}). A static transform needs neither: the secret
is applied offline (static obfuscation's re-keyed weights, served on unmodified vLLM) or on the
client (local DP's noised embeddings), so the server runs the stock model unchanged
(\cref{sec:obfuscation},~\cref{sec:dp}). A TEE is the middle case, running stock inference
software inside a confidential VM with attestation (\cref{sec:tee}). Stock-server
compatibility is one more advantage the static-transform families hold over the cryptographic and
hybrid ones.

\paragraph{Confidential accelerators are scarce and enterprise-only.}
The pure confidential-accelerator path, a GPU- or NPU-TEE trusted end to end, is the most
performant route that is also exact and near-plaintext in speed, but it trades the confidentiality
problem for a hardware-procurement one (\cref{sec:tee}). The confidential accelerators our
survey covers are the NVIDIA confidential-computing GPUs (the H100/H200/B200 class) and the
Huawei Ascend NPU; the platform space beyond these is narrow and still forming, since confidential GPU
support is recent, concentrated in high-end enterprise parts, and gained inline TEE-I/O only with
Blackwell (\cref{sec:tee}). Trusting such an accelerator end to end also binds a deployment to
one vendor's silicon and attestation. That cost is marginal for frontier generation,
where a high-end accelerator is needed anyway and confidentiality is the only added requirement,
but pure overhead for embedding or reranking, where a small encoder needs no high-end accelerator
yet no cheaper confidential accelerator exists to match the smaller workload. The scarcity is what the hybrid
split sidesteps by offloading to commodity untrusted GPUs (\cref{sec:hybrid}).

\paragraph{For heuristic schemes, a refreshed secret is what survives accumulation.}
The same failure recurs across three families. A single secret, reused on every request, lets
the adversary accumulate the externalities the scheme offloads (the obfuscated activations,
attention, and externalized KV-cache) until they recover what the secret was meant to hide. Fixed permutations are broken by prompt reconstruction
in cryptographic inference (\cref{sec:crypto}), static obfuscation accumulates into blind source
separation and learned inversion (\cref{sec:obfuscation}), and a reused KV-cache shuffle is
broken by collision (\cref{sec:hybrid}). In every case the schemes that survive are the ones that
refresh the secret: KV-Cloak draws a fresh permutation per cache block, GELO a fresh mixing
matrix per batch, and SCX a one-time key per generated token. Refreshing is necessary but not
sufficient. Under an orthogonal mixing the Gram matrix $U^{\top}U$ equals $H^{\top}H$ whichever
matrix is drawn, so a fresh secret per batch still exposes the hidden states' pairwise inner
products, which the blind-source-separation and anchor attacks of \cref{sec:hybrid} exploit. How
often to refresh under a latency budget is itself open (\cref{op:refresh-frequency}).

\paragraph{Malicious-operator security is an added integrity layer, not a free upgrade.}
Most schemes here assume an honest-but-curious operator; defending one that may also deviate
(drop, replay, or tamper) requires adding integrity, by one of two routes. The cryptographic
route adds authenticated computation: information-theoretic MACs make secret-sharing MPC actively
secure (the SPDZ line), TwinShield's U-Verify is a Freivalds-style check over a malicious GPU
(\cref{sec:hybrid}), and Opal authenticates memory against a malicious host with a Merkle tree
(\cref{sec:tee}). The hardware route adds attestation, as in the confidential accelerators
(Nvidia-CC, Ascend-CC). Malicious resistance is thus not hardware-exclusive, yet the surveyed
cryptographic schemes (SHAFT, Fission, PermLLM, CryptoMoE) take neither: all are proved only
semi-honest. MACs could secure their linear core, but the permutation-reveal optimizations 
that give them their speed lie outside what a MAC protects. 
The gap is real: malicious resistance is achievable
cryptographically, yet no surveyed crypto scheme pays for it, and the families that reach it rely
on integrity machinery or trusted hardware the semi-honest designs omit.

\paragraph{The field-wide side-channel blind spot.}
\label{ssec:sidechannel}
The residual-leakage axis of the taxonomy (\cref{tab:threat-taxonomy}) gives a uniform answer:
\emph{every} scheme places the microarchitectural, cache, timing, and physical side channels
(power analysis and the memory bus among them) out of scope. GELO excludes accelerator-side leakage beyond raw memory
reads~\citep{belikov_gelo}, Opal enumerates the in-enclave channels it does \emph{not}
defend~\citep{kaviani_opal}, and the rest omit them. This is a deliberate exclusion, and it
falls hardest on the TEE and hybrid families, whose entire trust is the silicon. Their
best-documented weaknesses are exactly microarchitectural and physical: transient-execution
attacks of the Spectre and Foreshadow class~\citep{spectre,foreshadow}, and physical memory-bus
interposition such as TEE.Fail, which extracts keys and forges attestation from the
deterministic memory encryption of server TEEs. The vendors classify such physical attacks as
out of scope, with no fix planned~\citep{tee_fail}. Individual attacks are patched on the platform cycle, but
the class recurs because each generation's performance optimizations reopen an observable
channel, and unlike a cryptographic assumption a hardware-anchored design cannot close it
without the vendor.

\paragraph{Graph-RAG is the most privacy-underserved setting.}
No surveyed work delivers an end-to-end confidential graph-RAG deployment, and it is the setting
the field, ours included, covers least. What this SoK offers is a per-component map of which
surveyed primitive serves which part of the graph pipeline (\cref{ssec:retr-graph}): entity and
relation ANN lookups by Compass, Pacmann, and CAPRISE; one-hop adjacency by the volume-hiding
multi-maps XorMM and FLASH; graph storage by PeGraph and GORAM; access hiding by Opal (TEE) or a client-side ORAM index;
and the cheapest, heuristic protection by the anonymization of PrivGemo and ARoG. One stage
stays uncovered across the corpus: building the graph index itself under confidentiality, before
any query runs. Confidential graph construction is the clearest open problem in this setting.
