\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Motivation}
Organizations in regulated domains increasingly want to run Transformer inference, both
embedding and generation, over confidential data: clinical notes, financial records, legal
files, proprietary code. The default path exposes exactly that data, because a hosted
inference or embedding API receives every document and query in plaintext, in reach of the
operator, a privileged insider, or anyone who breaches the service. GDPR, HIPAA, and the EU~AI
Act make this incompatible with processing personal, health, or privileged data, and
data-residency rules can forbid the egress outright. In a 2024 industry survey, half of
enterprises already adopted RAG to keep data in house~\citep{cloudera_ai_survey}, accepting
weaker self-hosted models because the strongest models are reachable only through such APIs.

Confidential inference aims to lift that constraint: to use an untrusted operator's
infrastructure, including frontier models and accelerators, without revealing the data to it.
The difficulty is protecting data \emph{in use}, not just at rest. On hardware it controls, the
operator sees the inputs and activations of every forward pass, and a RAG pipeline further
exposes the query, the access pattern over the corpus, and the assembled prompt, where the most
sensitive context is concentrated. The European Data Protection
Supervisor warns that RAG systems can disclose personal data held in their
corpus~\citep{edps_rag}, and a single poisoned document has been shown to turn a RAG
application into a remote data-exfiltration channel~\citep{greshake_ipi}.

A fast-growing body of work answers this with cryptography, trusted hardware, obfuscation,
differential privacy, and hybrids, spanning the inference stages (embedding, reranking,
generation) and the retrieval and storage layer. But the schemes assume different adversaries
and report costs on different hardware, models, and metrics, so a practitioner cannot tell
which is deployable. This systematization supplies that comparison.

\subsection{Scope and Positioning}
% Merged Why-a-New-SoK + Scope (goal-first). Positioning compressed: the table carries
% per-SoK detail; prose = one clause each + the sharp Andreoletti contrast.
Our organizing goal is \emph{deployment readiness}: how likely a scheme is to be adopted in
a real deployment, on three criteria: \textbf{performance} (overhead and latency),
\textbf{fidelity} (impact on output quality), and \textbf{threat-model fit} (an adversary
model \emph{appropriate} to the deployment, not maximal). The aim is not to crown a winning
family but to make this trade explicit; which schemes sit near the deployable frontier is a
\emph{finding} of the synthesis (\cref{sec:synthesis}), not an assumption. Threat-model fit
is set by the taxonomy of \cref{sec:threat}; performance and fidelity are reported per family
(\cref{sec:crypto}--\cref{sec:hybrid}) and drawn together in \cref{sec:synthesis}.

No prior survey occupies this ground (\cref{tab:prior-soks}); each covers a slice. A
risk-centric SoK catalogs attacks over naive RAG~\citep{bodea_sok}; a cluster of surveys
treats \emph{cryptographic} private inference, confined to
MPC/FHE~\citep{li_pti_survey,pti_survey_2024,luo_cplm_survey,chen_sok_pti}; others go deep on
a single FHE primitive~\citep{xue_fhe_sok,albadawi_ckks_sok} or stay high-level across the
lifecycle~\citep{ma_semantic_sok}. For MPC/FHE-inference protocol depth we \emph{refer the
reader to} \citet{li_pti_survey} and \citet{chen_sok_pti} (the latter
adding a reproducibility re-benchmark), keeping only a few frontier exemplars
(\cref{sec:crypto}). The one prior work sharing our deployability lens,
\citet{andreoletti_ppi_practice}, differs substantively: it is a \emph{meta}-survey at the
level of PET \emph{families} (qualitative trade-offs, thin on information-theoretic and
obfuscation methods, no retrieval or graph-RAG), and where it argues one trust-minimizing
trajectory (TEE\,$\to$\,FHE) we score \emph{individual schemes} and reach a different
conclusion: today's deployable options are present-day systems such as the hybrid split,
obfuscation, and confidential-accelerator TEEs, not way-stations toward a cryptographic
endpoint (\cref{ssec:deployment}). Our contribution is thus breadth across all five families,
per-scheme depth, the retrieval and graph-RAG dimension, and a quantitative
deployment-readiness lens.

Two structural choices follow. First, a \emph{hardware-trust} ladder, defined with the
trust-anchor axis of our taxonomy (\cref{sec:threat}), feeds the performance and
threat-fit criteria: Tier~1 (no trusted hardware), Tier~2 (CPU-TEE only, commodity GPU
untrusted), and Tier~3 (a confidential accelerator trusted end-to-end). We focus on
Tiers~1 and~2, where confidentiality is an open \emph{design} problem, and keep
\textbf{Tier~3} as a performance baseline (\cref{sec:tee}): once a confidential
accelerator is trusted end-to-end, confidentiality largely reduces to hardware
availability, enclave construction, and attestation, a narrower, hardware-specific
space systematized in its own right~\citep{accel_tee_sok}. Second, the
\textbf{practical-frontier} restriction follows directly from the deployment lens:
comparison tables hold only state-of-the-art schemes with deployable overhead, and
foundational or superseded work is cited for lineage only.

The systematization spans Transformer inference (embedding, reranking, generation) and
RAG including \emph{graph-RAG}, a setting the prior RAG-privacy SoK~\citep{bodea_sok}
excludes. We treat a semi-honest cloud as the baseline adversary, documenting the range
to malicious in \cref{sec:threat}. Explicitly \emph{out of scope}: microarchitectural
side-channels (a field-wide exclusion we make a finding of, \cref{sec:threat}), full
verifiable computation (lightweight composable integrity is in scope, but zkLLM-class
proving is not), foundational/superseded schemes, and \emph{on-device model-inference
protection}, schemes such as SecureInfer~\citep{secureinfer} and
Amulet~\citep{amulet} that defend a proprietary model against the device owner
(an inverted threat model: protecting the \emph{model} from the user, rather than the
user's data from the cloud), which is orthogonal to the confidential-RAG setting we study.

\subsection{Contributions}
This paper is a \emph{systematization of knowledge}. Concretely:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A five-axis threat-model taxonomy} (\cref{sec:threat}) that
        classifies confidential Transformer-inference and RAG schemes by
        adversary behavior, trust anchor, attacker knowledge, confidentiality
        scope, and \emph{security basis} (cryptographic / DP / hardware /
        heuristic), the last separating provably-secure schemes from
        empirically-resistant ones, and surfacing the field-wide side-channel blind
        spot we report as a finding in \cref{ssec:sidechannel}.
  \item \textbf{A mechanism-organized systematization} of five inference-defense
        families (\cref{sec:crypto}--\cref{sec:hybrid}) plus a dedicated treatment of the
        confidential \textbf{retrieval and storage} layer (\cref{sec:retrieval}), dense and
        graph/LightRAG, spanning the full inference-and-RAG pipeline.
  \item \textbf{A performance comparison} synthesized from reported numbers
        (\cref{sec:synthesis}), the cost axis the existing RAG-privacy SoK
        \citep{bodea_sok} omits, with an explicit non-comparability caveat.
  \item \textbf{A deployment-readiness synthesis} (\cref{sec:synthesis}) that scores
        each family on performance, fidelity, and threat-model fit, showing that
        real-world readiness comes from balancing the three rather than maximizing any
        one, and locating each family on that frontier.
  \item \textbf{A stage\,$\times$\,mechanism gap-map} (\cref{sec:synthesis})
        whose empty cells are the field's open problems.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Organization}
The evaluation framework (\cref{sec:framework}) defines the security taxonomy and the cost
metrics. The five mechanism families for confidential \emph{inference} are systematized in
\cref{sec:crypto}--\cref{sec:hybrid}; the RAG \emph{retrieval and storage} layer (dense and
graph/LightRAG, across all mechanisms) is treated separately in \cref{sec:retrieval}, since
its trade-offs and primitives differ. The synthesis (\cref{sec:synthesis}) draws them
together: the stage\,$\times$\,mechanism gap-map, the performance comparison, the
deployment-readiness profile, graph-RAG as a retrieval setting, and the field-wide
side-channel finding. \Cref{sec:related} positions the work against prior surveys, and the
conclusion lists open problems.
