\subsection{Why a New SoK}
% The field has many point solutions (crypto/MPC/FHE, TEE, static obfuscation,
% DP, hybrid split) but no systematization that unifies MECHANISM + THREAT MODEL
% + PERFORMANCE. The closest prior work, Bodea et al. \cite{bodea_sok}, is
% RISK-centric, scoped to NAIVE RAG, omits a performance axis, and treats
% crypto/TEE inference only shallowly. We occupy the complementary ground.
Privacy in confidential Transformer inference and RAG has drawn several recent surveys, but
each occupies a narrower slice than the mechanism\,$\times$\,threat-model\,$\times$\,cost ground we
systematize (\cref{tab:prior-soks}). The closest, Bodea et al.~\citep{bodea_sok}, is a
\emph{risk}-centric SoK of RAG privacy---it catalogs attacks and mitigations over naive RAG, but
carries no performance axis and explicitly excludes graph-RAG. A second cluster surveys
\emph{cryptographic} private Transformer inference: Li et al.~\citep{li_pti_survey}---the most
detailed, with a layer-wise MPC/HE taxonomy and a communication/runtime/accuracy table---an earlier
MPC/FHE/hybrid survey~\citep{pti_survey_2024}, Luo et al.~\citep{luo_cplm_survey} (a
cryptographic lifecycle view that also spans training), and Chen et al.~\citep{chen_sok_pti}, who
add a reproducibility audit that re-benchmarks 61 crypto frameworks under standardized hardware. All
of these are confined to MPC/FHE and to \emph{inference}, touching neither the TEE, obfuscation, and
DP families nor the RAG retrieval/storage layer. FHE-specific SoKs~\citep{xue_fhe_sok,albadawi_ckks_sok}
go deep on a single primitive, and broad lifecycle treatments~\citep{ma_semantic_sok} stay high-level
on defenses without a cost axis. Closest in \emph{spirit}, Andreoletti et
al.~\citep{andreoletti_ppi_practice} share our deployability lens, but theirs is a \emph{meta}-survey
organized at the level of PET \emph{families} (a few representative systems per family with qualitative
trade-off tables), not a per-scheme comparison; it is especially thin on information-theoretic and
obfuscation methods (its ``information-reduction'' family reduces to client-side DP and span redaction,
omitting the learned-transform/covariant-obfuscation lineage and its published inversions), and it
covers neither RAG retrieval nor graph-RAG.

We are \emph{complementary} to most of these and \emph{differ sharply} from the one that shares our
lens. No prior survey places all five mechanism families on one threat-model and deployment-cost
frame, and none covers private RAG retrieval, storage, or graph-RAG. For the fine-grained
systematization of MPC/FHE inference protocols we \emph{refer the reader to} Li et
al.~\citep{li_pti_survey} and Chen et al.~\citep{chen_sok_pti}, keeping only enough crypto-inference
depth---a few frontier exemplars (\cref{sec:crypto})---to locate cryptography on our cross-family cost
frontier. Against Andreoletti et al.~\citep{andreoletti_ppi_practice} the contrast is substantive, not
just of scope: where they survey families and argue a single trust-minimising trajectory
(TEE\,$\to$\,crypto-augmentation\,$\to$\,FHE), we score \emph{individual schemes} on a quantitative
deployment-readiness frontier and reach a different conclusion (\cref{ssec:deployment})---one in which
the hybrid split and obfuscation are present-day leaders rather than way-stations. Our distinctive
contribution is thus the breadth across mechanisms, the per-scheme depth, the retrieval and graph-RAG
dimension, and the quantitative deployment-readiness lens.

\subsection{Scope}
% Transformer inference AND RAG, including graph-RAG (a setting Bodea excludes).
% Five mechanism families. Semi-honest baseline (range to malicious documented in §2).
%
% ORGANIZING GOAL = DEPLOYMENT READINESS (decision of record 2026-06-02): assess the
% whole field by likelihood of real-world adoption on 3 criteria -- (1) performance,
% (2) fidelity (output quality), (3) threat-model FIT (appropriate, not maximal). We do
% NOT champion one family; hybrid-split/obfuscation lead as a FINDING (good perf-to-threat
% ratio), established in §8, not assumed here. Hardware-trust TIERS (Tier1 none / Tier2
% CPU-TEE / Tier3 confidential accelerator) feed criteria 1+3; Tier3 = baseline, deferred
% to accel_tee_sok. PRACTICAL-FRONTIER restriction = a consequence of the lens.
Our organizing goal is \emph{deployment readiness}: we assess the field by how likely a
scheme is to be adopted in a real deployment, on three criteria---\textbf{performance}
(overhead and latency), \textbf{fidelity} (impact on output accuracy and quality), and
\textbf{threat-model fit} (an adversary model \emph{appropriate} to the deployment, not
maximal in a purely academic sense). The aim is not to crown a winning family but to
make this trade explicit; which schemes sit near the deployable frontier is a
\emph{finding} of the synthesis (\cref{sec:synthesis}), not an assumption. Of the
three criteria, threat-model fit is determined by the categorical taxonomy of
\cref{sec:threat}, while performance and fidelity are quantitative---reported with
each family (\cref{sec:crypto}--\cref{sec:hybrid}) and drawn together in
\cref{sec:synthesis}.

Two structural choices follow. First, a \emph{hardware-trust} ladder, defined with the
trust-anchor axis of our taxonomy (\cref{sec:threat}), feeds the performance and
threat-fit criteria: Tier~1 (no trusted hardware), Tier~2 (CPU-TEE only, commodity GPU
untrusted), and Tier~3 (a confidential accelerator trusted end-to-end). We focus on
Tiers~1 and~2, where confidentiality is an open \emph{design} problem, and keep
\textbf{Tier~3} as a performance baseline (\cref{sec:tee}): once a confidential
accelerator is trusted end-to-end, confidentiality largely reduces to hardware
availability, enclave construction, and attestation---a narrower, hardware-specific
space systematized in its own right~\citep{accel_tee_sok}. Second, the
\textbf{practical-frontier} restriction follows directly from the deployment lens:
comparison tables hold only state-of-the-art schemes with deployable overhead, and
foundational or superseded work is cited for lineage only.

The systematization spans Transformer inference (embedding, reranking, generation) and
RAG including \emph{graph-RAG}---a setting the prior RAG-privacy SoK~\citep{bodea_sok}
excludes. We treat a semi-honest cloud as the baseline adversary, documenting the range
to malicious in \cref{sec:threat}. Explicitly \emph{out of scope}: microarchitectural
side-channels (a field-wide exclusion we make a finding of, \cref{sec:threat}), full
verifiable computation (lightweight composable integrity is in scope, but zkLLM-class
proving is not), foundational/superseded schemes, and \emph{on-device model-inference
protection}---schemes such as SecureInfer~\citep{secureinfer} and
Amulet~\citep{amulet} that defend a proprietary model against the device owner
(an inverted threat model: protecting the \emph{model} from the user, rather than the
user's data from the cloud), which is orthogonal to the confidential-RAG setting we study.

\subsection{Contributions}
This paper is a \emph{systematization of knowledge}. Concretely:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A five-axis threat-model taxonomy} (\cref{sec:threat}) that
        classifies confidential Transformer-inference and RAG schemes by
        adversary behavior, trust anchor, attacker knowledge, confidentiality
        scope, and \emph{security basis} (cryptographic / DP / hardware /
        heuristic) --- the last separating provably-secure schemes from
        empirically-resistant ones, and surfacing the field-wide side-channel blind
        spot we report as a finding in \cref{ssec:sidechannel}.
  \item \textbf{A mechanism-organized systematization} of five inference-defense
        families (\cref{sec:crypto}--\cref{sec:hybrid}) plus a dedicated treatment of the
        confidential \textbf{retrieval and storage} layer (\cref{sec:retrieval})---dense and
        graph/LightRAG---spanning the full inference-and-RAG pipeline.
  \item \textbf{A performance comparison} synthesized from reported numbers
        (\cref{sec:synthesis}) --- the cost axis the existing RAG-privacy SoK
        \cite{bodea_sok} omits --- with an explicit non-comparability caveat.
  \item \textbf{A deployment-readiness synthesis} (\cref{sec:synthesis}) that scores
        each family on performance, fidelity, and threat-model fit, showing that
        real-world readiness comes from balancing the three rather than maximizing any
        one --- and locating each family on that frontier.
  \item \textbf{A stage\,$\times$\,mechanism gap-map} (\cref{sec:synthesis})
        whose empty cells are the field's open problems.
  \item \textbf{A validation case study} (\cref{app:validation}): the reported
        resistance of deployment-ready heuristic-basis schemes does not hold under the
        realistic threat model. We report the \emph{finding} and a reproduction protocol;
        the attack methodology and a defense are deferred to a companion paper.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Organization}
The evaluation framework (\cref{sec:framework}) defines the security taxonomy and the cost
metrics. The five mechanism families for confidential \emph{inference} are systematized in
\cref{sec:crypto}--\cref{sec:hybrid}; the RAG \emph{retrieval and storage} layer---dense and
graph/LightRAG, across all mechanisms---is treated separately in \cref{sec:retrieval}, since
its trade-offs and primitives differ. The synthesis (\cref{sec:synthesis}) draws them
together---the stage\,$\times$\,mechanism gap-map, the performance comparison, the
deployment-readiness profile, graph-RAG as a retrieval setting, and the field-wide
side-channel finding. \Cref{sec:related} positions the work against prior surveys, and the
conclusion lists open problems.
