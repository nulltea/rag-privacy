\section{Cryptographic Approaches: MPC, FHE, and Secret Sharing}
\label{sec:crypto}

Cryptographic schemes compute on data the server never sees in the clear: secure
multi-party computation (MPC) and secret sharing (SS) split the computation across
parties, while fully-homomorphic encryption (FHE) evaluates directly on ciphertext.
They offer the strongest confidentiality in our taxonomy (\cref{sec:threat})---a
trusted computing base of \emph{none} (FHE) or \emph{non-colluding parties}
(MPC/SS), with no special hardware---at the cost of one-to-three orders of magnitude
in latency or communication, concentrated in the non-linear operations (Softmax,
GELU, LayerNorm). We separate \emph{private inference} (\cref{ssec:crypto-inf}) from \emph{private retrieval
and storage} (\cref{ssec:crypto-retrieval})---the RAG-side use that is closest to deployable
today. Because MPC/FHE inference protocols are the subject of dedicated recent
surveys~\citep{li_pti_survey,pti_survey_2024}, we cover that sub-family briefly---through a few
frontier exemplars---and focus on cryptographic retrieval and storage,
which those surveys do not cover.
On the deployment-readiness criteria, cryptography scores highest on
threat-model fit and fidelity (exact computation under a TCB of none or non-collusion)
but lowest on performance for inference; its retrieval variants fare materially better
(\cref{sec:synthesis}).

\subsection{Approach}
\label{ssec:crypto-approach}
Cryptographic confidentiality for an LLM/RAG pipeline is bought by two families of
primitive that address two distinct leakages: \emph{compute-hiding} primitives conceal
the values a server computes on, and \emph{access-hiding} primitives conceal which data
a server touches.

\paragraph{Compute-hiding (inference).}
Secret sharing (SS) and the multi-party protocols built on it (MPC) split each value
into shares held by non-colluding parties. Linear maps---the bulk of a Transformer's
matrix multiplications---are then local (free under SS) or need only cheap Beaver-triple
multiplications, so the cost concentrates entirely in the non-linear operations. Softmax,
GELU, and LayerNorm have no linear-sharing analogue and must be evaluated through
oblivious transfer or garbled circuits, or replaced by polynomial/Fourier
approximations---each adding rounds, communication, or approximation error.
Fully-homomorphic encryption (FHE) instead evaluates a circuit directly on ciphertext
under a single key; the RNS-CKKS scheme used in most FHE-based Transformer inference is
\emph{leveled}, supporting computation only up to a fixed multiplicative depth $L$ before an expensive
bootstrap, which again forces the non-linearities into low-degree polynomial
approximations that consume the depth budget. (An alternative FHE line evaluates the
non-linearities as lookup tables via programmable bootstrapping in TFHE-style schemes; the
leveled-RNS-CKKS exemplars we cover do not use it, and we follow the dedicated inference
surveys~\citep{li_pti_survey} in not exemplifying it separately.) In both families the
non-linear operations are the bottleneck---Li et al.~\citep{li_pti_survey} measure them at
more than $85\%$ of total runtime for MPC and around $60\%$ for HE---and this is the
structural reason secure inference sits one-to-three orders of magnitude above plaintext
(\cref{ssec:crypto-inf}).

\paragraph{Access-hiding (retrieval).}
Retrieval leaks through a different channel---\emph{which} vectors or graph nodes a query
touches. Private information retrieval (PIR) lets a client fetch a record without the
server learning which one; oblivious RAM (ORAM) hides the access pattern of a sequence of
reads and writes; searchable/structured encryption (SSE) and encrypted multi-maps (EMM)
support keyword and adjacency lookups over ciphertext while bounding what access and
volume patterns reveal; and distance-comparison-preserving encryption (DCPE) keeps
similarity search cheap by encrypting vectors so that distance \emph{comparisons}
survive---trading exactness and a quantified leakage for near-plaintext speed. These
primitives compose with the compute-hiding layer but are costed and attacked separately,
which is why we tabulate retrieval (\cref{ssec:crypto-retrieval}) apart from inference.

\subsection{Cryptographic Inference}
\label{ssec:crypto-inf}
We do not re-systematize MPC/FHE Transformer-inference protocols---Li et
al.~\citep{li_pti_survey} survey that space in depth (layer-wise, MPC-vs-HE). We keep five
frontier exemplars (\cref{tab:crypto-inf}) chosen to span the design space: a two-party
protocol (SHAFT), a 2PC protocol with an offline dealer (PermLLM), distributed MPC with
evaluator nodes (Fission), private mixture-of-experts routing (CryptoMoE), and
non-interactive FHE (Euston). Together they locate cryptography on our deployment-cost
frontier. CryptoMoE is the one exemplar whose contribution is architecture-specific:
naive private mixture-of-experts inference must either evaluate \emph{every} expert
(forfeiting the sparsity that motivates MoE) or reveal which experts each token selects.
Because experts specialize by semantic domain, that routing pattern leaks the input's
type---and expert-choice routing has been exploited concretely to recover a co-batched
user's prompt in full~\citep{yona_moe_steal}. CryptoMoE therefore keeps the routing
secret-shared and balances expert load so per-expert computation is data-independent,
recovering MoE sparsity under MPC at $2.8$--$3.5\times$ the dense-MPC cost (evaluated on
OLMoE, QWenMoE, and DeepSeekMoE at $6.9$--$16.4$B total, $1.3$--$2.8$B active parameters). The pattern across all five is uniform: even the fastest exchanges hundreds of MB to
GBs per token or inference, at exact (MPC) or near-exact (FHE) fidelity. None of these
papers reports overhead against plaintext---they benchmark against prior \emph{secure}
schemes, and Li et al.\ likewise do not normalize to plaintext: they tabulate each
scheme's self-reported latency, communication, and task accuracy, anchored to a common
model (BERT-base) where possible. The absolute figures in \cref{tab:crypto-inf} are
therefore paper-reported where available and otherwise recovered by one-hop extrapolation
(\cref{sec:comparison}).

Retrieval cryptography is the practical frontier of this family, and the schemes divide by
\emph{what} they hide. We group them by leakage channel; each group differs in mechanism,
threat model, and what drives its cost.

\paragraph{Access-hiding (PIR/ORAM).}
Pacmann, Panther, and PIR-RAG answer a query through private information retrieval, so the
server returns the matching record(s) without learning \emph{which} were touched; Compass
goes further, wrapping an encrypted HNSW index in client-side ORAM to hide the access
\emph{sequence} even against a \emph{malicious} server, leaving only the operation type
(search vs.\ insert/delete) visible. The cost driver is the obliviousness itself: PIR keeps
communication sublinear but forces a server-side linear scan, and ORAM adds a
logarithmic-factor access overhead---which is why these run in seconds at million-scale
rather than milliseconds. For graph-RAG, Pacmann and Compass fit the entity/relation ANN
lookups that open a LightRAG-style retrieval, hiding which node a query resolves to. This
obliviousness carries a deployment cost the ``no trusted hardware'' label hides: the client
is \emph{load-bearing}---Compass runs the ORAM controller and holds its position map,
Pacmann performs the graph traversal client-side---so the scheme relieves the server of
trust but not the user of work. We treat this client-reliance as a first-order deployment
axis in \cref{sec:synthesis}.

\paragraph{Volume-bounding (SSE/EMM).}
XorMM, FLASH, and PeGraph encrypt the adjacency and keyword multi-maps that graph retrieval
walks, returning postings while padding result-set size so the query-result \emph{volume}
does not leak. This is precisely the LightRAG adjacency and \texttt{source\_id} fan-out
surface: entity$\to$relation and chunk$\to$source lookups are volume-leaking multi-map
queries, and volume-hiding EMMs bound that leak at a $1.5$--$3\times$ storage cost (the
conjunctive variant FLASH adds a keyword-pair access pattern of its own). What remains is
the search/query-equality pattern, not the content---closed only by layering ORAM (as
Compass does) or rotating keys per session.

\paragraph{Distance-comparison-preserving encryption (DCPE).}
SAP/DCPE and CAPRISE take the opposite trade: a symmetric per-vector transform
($c = s\,e + \lambda$, the same dimension as the plaintext embedding---so the ciphertext
imposes ${\approx}1\times$ storage, and the papers report no blow-up because there is none)
that preserves \emph{distance comparisons}, letting the server rank encrypted database
vectors by similarity to an encrypted query at near-plaintext speed (CAPRISE encrypts
2339\,vectors/s) with no interaction and no homomorphic operations. The two differ in
\emph{which} comparisons survive: SAP~\citep{fuchsbauer_sap} preserves \emph{all} pairwise
comparisons, including those among database vectors, which leaks the corpus's relational
structure to a vector-analysis attack; CAPRISE~\citep{ye_caprise} is \emph{conditional}---by
adding asymmetric noise to query versus database encryptions it preserves only
query-to-document comparisons while obfuscating inter-document ones, and perturbs the query
under differential privacy, closing the structural leak SAP leaves open. The shared price is
exactness and a quantified leakage---the preserved comparison structure is
invertible---which we examine in \cref{ssec:crypto-limits} (with the obfuscation parallel in
\cref{sec:obfuscation}).

\paragraph{Why retrieval, not inference, is the deployable frontier.}
The two halves of this section sit one-to-three orders of magnitude apart. Access- and
volume-hiding retrieval answers million-scale queries in sub-second to seconds (Compass
sub-second; PeGraph ${<}1$\,s; CAPRISE 2339\,vec/s), because it hides \emph{access} over a
mostly-plaintext-speed index rather than computing Transformer non-linearities under
encryption as the inference exemplars must. For graph-RAG specifically, the kept set covers
LightRAG's actual retrieval surface---entity/relation/chunk ANN (Compass, Pacmann, CAPRISE),
one-hop adjacency expansion (XorMM, FLASH), and graph storage (PeGraph, GORAM)---which is
why this family, not crypto inference, anchors the deployable end of the cryptographic
design space (\cref{sec:synthesis}). Because no scheme reports a vs-plaintext multiplier and
hardware, models, and datasets differ across rows, the tabulated figures are indicative, not
comparable (\cref{sec:comparison}).

\subsection{Limitations and Known Attacks}
\label{ssec:crypto-limits}

\paragraph{The trust assumption is the security.}
Every cryptographic scheme in this section is proved secure only against a
\emph{semi-honest} adversary, and the MPC/SS schemes additionally require their computing
parties \emph{not to collude}. Neither assumption is free. Muse shows that a single
\emph{malicious} client deviating from a semi-honest 2PC inference protocol can extract
the server's model weights~\citep{muse_2021}, and two colluding parties---or, for the
2-server retrieval schemes p\textsuperscript{2}RAG and PeGraph, two co-located
servers---collapse additive sharing back to plaintext. Closing the malicious-client gap
costs roughly $1.3$--$2\times$ (SIMC, secure inference resilient to malicious
clients~\citep{simc_2022}) and full malicious-secure inference more; no scheme we survey
adopts either. The deployment-readiness consequence is sharp: under a realistic
cloud adversary that can deviate or quietly co-locate its ``non-colluding'' parties, the
cryptographic guarantee degrades to that of an honest operator---comparable in trust to a
TEE, but without the attestation that lets a client \emph{verify} it
(\cref{sec:tee} and~\cref{sec:hybrid}).

\paragraph{Permutation secrecy is heuristic, not information-theoretic.}
The fastest secure-inference exemplars buy their speed by evaluating non-linearities on
\emph{permuted} plaintext rather than under MPC: PermLLM reveals randomly permuted hidden
states for in-clear evaluation, and Fission reveals shuffled activation \emph{chunks} to
evaluator nodes. Both papers concede this is not information-theoretic---PermLLM cannot
achieve provable secrecy, and Fission's own attack recovers $68.7\%$ binary-class
accuracy from PermLLM-style permuted reveals (versus $94.1\%$ with no permutation). Hidden
No More sharpens the point, reconstructing prompts with near-perfect accuracy from fixed
permutations by exploiting decoder injectivity---breaking STIP, Centaur, and PermLLM in a
third-party-node deployment~\citep{hnm}; the co-designed defence (token sharding) is only
\emph{statistically} secure, and only for some sharding choices~\citep{cascade_thomas}.
The exposure is contingent on \emph{who} sees the permuted reveal---PermLLM as specified
reveals to the user (their own data), and Fission's multi-evaluator chunking drives
leakage down as ${\sim}\log(N)$ but eliminates it only by batching queries---so
permutation schemes are best read as a heuristic speed/leakage trade, not a
confidentiality guarantee on par with full MPC or FHE.

\paragraph{FHE: depth budget and approximate-ciphertext security.}
RNS-CKKS supports only a bounded multiplicative depth before bootstrapping, so the
non-linearities are polynomial approximations whose accuracy is range-dependent (direct
Taylor approximations break on large input ranges). Separately, approximate-FHE
ciphertexts carry a security subtlety: the IND-CPA\textsuperscript{D} attack of Li and
Micciancio recovers a CKKS secret key when an adversary can \emph{observe decryptions} of
honestly evaluated ciphertexts~\citep{li_micciancio_2021}, mitigated by noise flooding at
some precision cost. The attack bites only where decrypted outputs cross a boundary the
adversary controls; for the non-interactive inference schemes here, where the server
never sees a decryption, plain IND-CPA suffices---but RAG deployments that return
decrypted scores or rankings to a queryable client should apply the countermeasure.

\paragraph{Retrieval: distance comparison, access, and volume leakage.}
The retrieval schemes trade exactness or residual leakage for their speed.
Distance-comparison-preserving encryption (SAP/DCPE, CAPRISE) preserves distance
\emph{comparisons} on ciphertext by construction---which is precisely the leak: the
property-preserving-encryption family is invertible from ciphertext plus auxiliary
distribution~\citep{naveed_ppe_2015}, and embeddings recovered this way are readable by
inversion models such as Vec2Text~\citep{morris2023vec2text} (CAPRISE obfuscates
inter-document distances and adds query-side noise specifically to blunt this). The
PIR/ORAM and searchable-encryption retrieval schemes (Panther, Pacmann, Compass; XorMM,
FLASH, PeGraph) leak instead through access and volume---``ANN'' is a search method, not a
scheme, so the leakage is the index's, not the search's: generic reconstruction attacks
consume access-pattern and communication-volume leakage~\citep{kellaris2016leakage},
$k$-nearest-neighbour query leakage alone suffices to recover
values~\citep{kornaropoulos_knn_2019}, and volume-only attacks defeat schemes that hide
\emph{which} record is touched but not \emph{how many}~\citep{blackstone_revisiting_2020}
---the reason volume-hiding multi-maps exist, though even those cannot asymptotically beat
naive padding~\citep{ando_cost_2022}. Crucially, this attack chain stops at the oblivious
schemes: PIR and ORAM (Pacmann, Panther, Compass) genuinely close the access-pattern
channel rather than merely leaving it unattacked, which is why they---not the
distance-preserving shortcuts---anchor the deployable end of cryptographic retrieval
(\cref{sec:synthesis}).
