\section{Cryptographic Approaches: MPC, FHE, and Secret Sharing}
\label{sec:crypto}

Cryptographic schemes compute on data the server never sees in the clear: secure
multi-party computation (MPC) and secret sharing (SS) split the computation across
parties, while fully-homomorphic encryption (FHE) evaluates directly on ciphertext.
They offer the strongest confidentiality in our taxonomy (\cref{sec:threat})---a
trusted computing base of \emph{none} (FHE) or \emph{non-colluding parties}
(MPC/SS), with no special hardware---at the cost of one-to-three orders of magnitude
in latency or communication, concentrated in the non-linear operations (Softmax,
GELU, LayerNorm). We separate \emph{private inference} (\cref{ssec:crypto-inf}) from \emph{private retrieval
and storage} (\cref{ssec:crypto-retrieval})---the RAG-side use that is closest to deployable
today. Because MPC/FHE inference protocols are the subject of dedicated recent
surveys~\citep{li_pti_survey,pti_survey_2024}, we treat that sub-family briefly---through a few
frontier exemplars---and focus on cryptographic retrieval and storage,
which those surveys do not cover.
On the deployment-readiness criteria, cryptography scores highest on
threat-model fit and fidelity (exact computation under a TCB of none or non-collusion)
but lowest on performance for inference; its retrieval variants fare materially better
(\cref{sec:synthesis}).

\subsection{Approach}
\label{ssec:crypto-approach}
Cryptographic confidentiality for an LLM/RAG pipeline is bought by two families of
primitive that address two distinct leakages: \emph{compute-hiding} primitives conceal
the values a server computes on, and \emph{access-hiding} primitives conceal which data
a server touches.

\paragraph{Compute-hiding (inference).}
Secret sharing (SS) and the multi-party protocols built on it (MPC) split each value
into shares held by non-colluding parties. Linear maps---the bulk of a Transformer's
matrix multiplications---are then local (free under SS) or need only cheap Beaver-triple
multiplications, so the cost concentrates entirely in the non-linear operations. Softmax,
GELU, and LayerNorm have no linear-sharing analogue and must be evaluated through
oblivious transfer or garbled circuits, or replaced by polynomial/Fourier
approximations---each adding rounds, communication, or approximation error.
Fully-homomorphic encryption (FHE) instead evaluates a circuit directly on ciphertext
under a single key; the RNS-CKKS scheme used by Transformer-FHE work is \emph{leveled},
supporting computation only up to a fixed multiplicative depth $L$ before an expensive
bootstrap, which again forces the non-linearities into low-degree polynomial
approximations that consume the depth budget. In both families the non-linear operations
are the bottleneck, and this is the structural reason secure inference sits one-to-three
orders of magnitude above plaintext (\cref{ssec:crypto-inf}).

\paragraph{Access-hiding (retrieval).}
Retrieval leaks through a different channel---\emph{which} vectors or graph nodes a query
touches. Private information retrieval (PIR) lets a client fetch a record without the
server learning which one; oblivious RAM (ORAM) hides the access pattern of a sequence of
reads and writes; searchable/structured encryption (SSE) and encrypted multi-maps (EMM)
support keyword and adjacency lookups over ciphertext while bounding what access and
volume patterns reveal; and distance-comparison-preserving encryption (DCPE) keeps
similarity search cheap by encrypting vectors so that distance \emph{orderings}
survive---trading exactness and a quantified leakage for near-plaintext speed. These
primitives compose with the compute-hiding layer but are costed and attacked separately,
which is why we tabulate retrieval (\cref{ssec:crypto-retrieval}) apart from inference.

\subsection{Cryptographic Inference (Exemplars; Depth Deferred)}

al.~\citep{li_pti_survey} survey that space in depth (layer-wise, MPC-vs-HE). We keep five
frontier exemplars (\cref{tab:crypto-inf}) that span the design space---2PC, 2PC with an

Retrieval cryptography is the practical frontier of this family, and the schemes divide
by what they hide. \emph{Access-hiding} retrieval closes the channel of which record is
touched: Pacmann and PIR-RAG answer a query through PIR without the server learning the
target, and Compass wraps an encrypted HNSW index in client-side ORAM to defend even a
\emph{malicious} server, leaving only the operation type (search vs.\ insert/delete)
visible. \emph{Volume-bounding} retrieval addresses the adjacency and multi-map leak
central to graph-RAG: XorMM and FLASH are volume-hiding encrypted multi-maps that hide
result-set size for entity/relation lookups, while PeGraph and GORAM extend encrypted
search to graph traversal at scale (\cref{sec:synthesis}). At the opposite end,
distance-comparison-preserving encryption (SAP/DCPE, CAPRISE) buys near-zero overhead
($\sim$2339\,vec/s) by preserving distance orderings directly on ciphertext---but that
preserved ordering is itself a leakage surface and the family is invertible; we treat the
attack in \cref{ssec:crypto-limits} (with the obfuscation parallel in
\cref{sec:obfuscation}).

\subsection{Performance (Reported)}
\label{ssec:crypto-perf}
Two performance regimes separate within this family, and the gap between them is the
section's deployment-readiness message. \emph{Inference} crypto remains one-to-three
orders of magnitude above plaintext: among the kept exemplars, PermLLM generates a token
in ${\approx}3$\,s (ChatGLM-6B, 1\,Gbps), CryptoMoE runs $22$--$46$\,s/token end-to-end
at $6.9$--$16.4$B parameters, and the non-interactive FHE path (Euston,
${\approx}10.7$\,s/inference extrapolated via NEXUS) trades interaction for
latency---all exchanging tens of Mb to several GB per token or inference, at exact (MPC)
or near-exact (FHE) fidelity (\cref{tab:crypto-inf}). \emph{Retrieval} crypto is
materially closer to practical: Compass answers encrypted graph-ANN queries in
sub-second LAN time, CAPRISE encrypts $2339$\,vectors/s, and the volume-hiding multi-maps
add only a $1.5$--$3\times$ storage factor (\cref{tab:crypto-vec} and~\cref{tab:crypto-graph}).
Because no scheme reports a vs-plaintext multiplier and the hardware, models, and
datasets differ across rows, these figures are indicative rather than comparable
(\cref{sec:comparison}); the cross-family placement is deferred to \cref{sec:synthesis}.

\subsection{Limitations and Known Attacks}
\label{ssec:crypto-limits}

\paragraph{The trust assumption is the security.}
Every cryptographic scheme in this section is proved secure only against a
\emph{semi-honest} adversary, and the MPC/SS schemes additionally require their computing
parties \emph{not to collude}. Neither assumption is free. Muse shows that a single
\emph{malicious} client deviating from a semi-honest 2PC inference protocol can extract
the server's model weights~\citep{muse_2021}, and two colluding parties---or, for the
2-server retrieval schemes p\textsuperscript{2}RAG and PeGraph, two co-located
servers---collapse additive sharing back to plaintext. Closing the malicious-client gap
costs roughly $1.3$--$2\times$ (SIMC) and full malicious-secure inference more; no scheme
we survey adopts either. The deployment-readiness consequence is sharp: under a realistic
cloud adversary that can deviate or quietly co-locate its ``non-colluding'' parties, the
cryptographic guarantee degrades to that of an honest operator---comparable in trust to a
TEE, but without the attestation that lets a client \emph{verify} it
(\cref{sec:tee} and~\cref{sec:hybrid}).

\paragraph{Permutation secrecy is heuristic, not information-theoretic.}
The fastest secure-inference exemplars buy their speed by evaluating non-linearities on
\emph{permuted} plaintext rather than under MPC: PermLLM reveals randomly permuted hidden
states for in-clear evaluation, and Fission reveals shuffled activation \emph{chunks} to
evaluator nodes. Both papers concede this is not information-theoretic---PermLLM cannot
achieve provable secrecy, and Fission's own attack recovers $68.7\%$ binary-class
accuracy from PermLLM-style permuted reveals (versus $94.1\%$ with no permutation). Hidden
No More sharpens the point, reconstructing prompts with near-perfect accuracy from fixed
permutations by exploiting decoder injectivity---breaking STIP, Centaur, and PermLLM in a
third-party-node deployment~\citep{hnm}; the co-designed defence (token sharding) is only
\emph{statistically} secure, and only for some sharding choices~\citep{cascade_thomas}.
The exposure is contingent on \emph{who} sees the permuted reveal---PermLLM as specified
reveals to the user (their own data), and Fission's multi-evaluator chunking drives
leakage down as ${\sim}\log(N)$ but eliminates it only by batching queries---so
permutation schemes are best read as a heuristic speed/leakage trade, not a
confidentiality guarantee on par with full MPC or FHE.

\paragraph{FHE: depth budget and approximate-ciphertext security.}
RNS-CKKS supports only a bounded multiplicative depth before bootstrapping, so the
non-linearities are polynomial approximations whose accuracy is range-dependent (direct
Taylor approximations break on large input ranges). Separately, approximate-FHE
ciphertexts carry a security subtlety: the IND-CPA\textsuperscript{D} attack of Li and
Micciancio recovers a CKKS secret key when an adversary can \emph{observe decryptions} of
honestly evaluated ciphertexts~\citep{li_micciancio_2021}, mitigated by noise flooding at
some precision cost. The attack bites only where decrypted outputs cross a boundary the
adversary controls; for the non-interactive inference schemes here, where the server
never sees a decryption, plain IND-CPA suffices---but RAG deployments that return
decrypted scores or rankings to a queryable client should apply the countermeasure.

\paragraph{Retrieval: ordering, access, and volume leakage.}
The retrieval schemes trade exactness or residual leakage for their speed.
Distance-comparison-preserving encryption (SAP/DCPE, CAPRISE) preserves distance
\emph{orderings} on ciphertext by construction---which is precisely the leak: the
property-preserving-encryption family is invertible from ciphertext plus auxiliary
distribution~\citep{naveed_ppe_2015}, and embeddings recovered this way are readable by
inversion models such as Vec2Text~\citep{morris2023vec2text} (CAPRISE obfuscates
inter-document distances and adds query-side noise specifically to blunt this). The
ANN/SSE schemes leak instead through access and volume: generic reconstruction attacks
consume access-pattern and communication-volume leakage~\citep{kellaris2016leakage},
$k$-nearest-neighbour query leakage alone suffices to recover
values~\citep{kornaropoulos_knn_2019}, and volume-only attacks defeat schemes that hide
\emph{which} record is touched but not \emph{how many}~\citep{blackstone_revisiting_2020}
---the reason volume-hiding multi-maps exist, though even those cannot asymptotically beat
naive padding~\citep{ando_cost_2022}. Crucially, this attack chain stops at the oblivious
schemes: PIR and ORAM (Pacmann, Panther, Compass) genuinely close the access-pattern
channel rather than merely leaving it unattacked, which is why they---not the
distance-preserving shortcuts---anchor the deployable end of cryptographic retrieval
(\cref{sec:synthesis}).
