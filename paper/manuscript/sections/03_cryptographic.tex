\section{Cryptographic Inference: MPC, FHE, and Secret Sharing}
\label{sec:crypto}

% Mechanism-section template (consistent across §3-§7):
%   Approach · Representative schemes (table) · Limitations / known attacks.
% Scope: INFERENCE only. The cryptographic RAG retrieval/storage layer is §8 (sec:retrieval).

Cryptographic schemes compute on data the server never sees in the clear: secure
multi-party computation (MPC) and secret sharing (SS) split the computation across
parties, while fully-homomorphic encryption (FHE) evaluates directly on ciphertext.
They offer the strongest confidentiality in our taxonomy (\cref{sec:threat})---a
trusted computing base of \emph{none} (FHE) or \emph{non-colluding parties}
(MPC/SS), with no special hardware---at the cost of one-to-three orders of magnitude
in latency or communication, concentrated in the non-linear operations (Softmax,
GELU, LayerNorm). This section covers cryptographic \emph{inference}; the cryptographic
machinery for the RAG retrieval and storage layer---PIR, ORAM, searchable encryption, and
distance-comparison-preserving encryption---is a distinct design space with different
trade-offs, and we treat it separately in \cref{sec:retrieval}. Because MPC/FHE inference
protocols are the subject of dedicated recent surveys~\citep{li_pti_survey,pti_survey_2024,chen_sok_pti},
we do not re-systematize the protocols themselves---Li et al.~\citep{li_pti_survey} survey
that space in depth (layer-wise, MPC-vs-HE), and Chen et al.~\citep{chen_sok_pti} add a
reproducibility re-benchmark of the same protocol space. We cover the sub-family briefly, through a few
frontier exemplars (\cref{tab:inf-perf}) that locate it on our deployment-cost frontier.
On the deployment-readiness criteria, cryptographic inference
scores highest on threat-model fit and fidelity (exact computation under a TCB of none or
non-collusion) but lowest on performance (\cref{sec:synthesis}).

\subsection{Approach}
\label{ssec:crypto-approach}
\emph{Secret sharing and MPC.} Secret sharing (SS) and the multi-party protocols built on
it (MPC) split each value into shares held by non-colluding parties. Linear maps---the bulk
of a Transformer's matrix multiplications---are then local (free under SS) or need only
cheap Beaver-triple multiplications, so the cost concentrates entirely in the non-linear
operations. Softmax, GELU, and LayerNorm have no linear-sharing analogue and must be
evaluated through oblivious transfer or garbled circuits, or replaced by polynomial/Fourier
approximations---each adding rounds, communication, or approximation error.

\emph{Fully-homomorphic encryption.} FHE instead evaluates a circuit directly on ciphertext
under a single key; the RNS-CKKS scheme used in most FHE-based Transformer inference is
\emph{leveled}, supporting computation only up to a fixed multiplicative depth $L$ before an expensive
bootstrap, which again forces the non-linearities into low-degree polynomial
approximations that consume the depth budget. (An alternative FHE line evaluates the
non-linearities as lookup tables via programmable bootstrapping in TFHE-style schemes; the
leveled-RNS-CKKS exemplars we cover do not use it, and we follow the dedicated inference
surveys~\citep{li_pti_survey} in not exemplifying it separately.) In both families the
non-linear operations are the bottleneck---Li et al.~\citep{li_pti_survey} measure them at
more than $85\%$ of total runtime for MPC and around $60\%$ for HE---and this is the
structural reason secure inference sits one-to-three orders of magnitude above plaintext.
Autoregressive decoding compounds it: each generated token is a full forward pass, so any
per-pass secure overhead (and any per-token interaction round) is multiplied by the
generation length~\citep{andreoletti_ppi_practice}---the reason these schemes report
seconds-to-minutes \emph{per token}.
The complementary \emph{access-hiding} primitives (PIR, ORAM,
searchable encryption, distance-preserving encryption) that protect the RAG retrieval
layer are introduced in \cref{sec:retrieval}.

We keep five
frontier exemplars (\cref{tab:inf-perf}) chosen to span the design space: a two-party
protocol (SHAFT), a 2PC protocol with an offline dealer (PermLLM), distributed MPC with
evaluator nodes (Fission), private mixture-of-experts routing (CryptoMoE), and
non-interactive FHE (Euston). Together they locate cryptography on our deployment-cost
frontier. CryptoMoE is the one exemplar whose contribution is architecture-specific:
naive private mixture-of-experts inference must either evaluate \emph{every} expert
(forfeiting the sparsity that motivates MoE) or reveal which experts each token selects.
Because experts specialize by semantic domain, that routing pattern leaks the input's
type---and expert-choice routing has been exploited concretely to recover a co-batched
user's prompt in full~\citep{yona_moe_steal}. CryptoMoE therefore keeps the routing
secret-shared and balances expert load so per-expert computation is data-independent,
recovering MoE sparsity under MPC at $2.8$--$3.5\times$ the dense-MPC cost (evaluated on
OLMoE, QWenMoE, and DeepSeekMoE at $6.9$--$16.4$B total, $1.3$--$2.8$B active parameters). The pattern across all five is uniform: even the fastest exchanges hundreds of MB to
GBs per token or inference, at exact (MPC) or near-exact (FHE) fidelity. None of these
papers reports overhead against plaintext---they benchmark against prior \emph{secure}
schemes, and Li et al.\ likewise do not normalize to plaintext: they tabulate each
scheme's self-reported latency, communication, and task accuracy, anchored to a common
model (BERT-base) where possible. The absolute figures in \cref{tab:inf-perf} are
therefore paper-reported where available and otherwise recovered by one-hop extrapolation
(\cref{sec:comparison}).

\subsection{Limitations and Known Attacks}
\label{ssec:crypto-limits}
Each failure mode below is a concrete attack on a specific trust assumption or speed
heuristic; \cref{tab:crypto-attacks} collects them by family (MPC/secret sharing vs FHE).

\paragraph{The trust assumption is the security.}
Every cryptographic scheme in this section is proved secure only against a
\emph{semi-honest} adversary, and the MPC/SS schemes additionally require their computing
parties \emph{not to collude}. Neither assumption is free. Muse shows that a single
\emph{malicious} client deviating from a semi-honest 2PC inference protocol can extract
the server's model weights~\citep{muse_2021}, and two colluding parties (or two co-located
servers in a 2-server deployment) collapse additive sharing back to plaintext. Closing the malicious-client gap
costs roughly $1.3$--$2\times$ (SIMC, secure inference resilient to malicious
clients~\citep{simc_2022}) and full malicious-secure inference more; no scheme we survey
adopts either. The deployment-readiness consequence is sharp: under a realistic
cloud adversary that can deviate or quietly co-locate its ``non-colluding'' parties, the
cryptographic guarantee degrades to that of an honest operator---comparable in trust to a
TEE, but without the attestation that lets a client \emph{verify} it
(\cref{sec:tee} and~\cref{sec:hybrid}).

\paragraph{Permutation secrecy is heuristic, not information-theoretic.}
The fastest secure-inference exemplars buy their speed by evaluating non-linearities on
\emph{permuted} plaintext rather than under MPC: PermLLM reveals randomly permuted hidden
states for in-clear evaluation, and Fission reveals shuffled activation \emph{chunks} to
evaluator nodes. Both papers concede this is not information-theoretic---PermLLM cannot
achieve provable secrecy, and Fission's own attack recovers $68.7\%$ binary-class
accuracy from PermLLM-style permuted reveals (versus $94.1\%$ with no permutation). Hidden
No More sharpens the point, reconstructing prompts with near-perfect accuracy from fixed
permutations by exploiting decoder injectivity---breaking STIP, Centaur, and PermLLM in a
third-party-node deployment~\citep{hnm}; the co-designed defence (token sharding) is only
\emph{statistically} secure, and only for some sharding choices~\citep{cascade_thomas}.
The exposure is contingent on \emph{who} sees the permuted reveal---PermLLM as specified
reveals to the user (their own data), and Fission's multi-evaluator chunking drives
leakage down as ${\sim}\log(N)$ but eliminates it only by batching queries---so
permutation schemes are best read as a heuristic speed/leakage trade, not a
confidentiality guarantee on par with full MPC or FHE.

\paragraph{FHE: depth budget and approximate-ciphertext security.}
RNS-CKKS supports only a bounded multiplicative depth before bootstrapping, so the
non-linearities are polynomial approximations whose accuracy is range-dependent (direct
Taylor approximations break on large input ranges). Separately, approximate-FHE
ciphertexts carry a security subtlety: the IND-CPA\textsuperscript{D} attack of Li and
Micciancio recovers a CKKS secret key when an adversary can \emph{observe decryptions} of
honestly evaluated ciphertexts~\citep{li_micciancio_2021}, mitigated by noise flooding at
some precision cost. The attack bites only where decrypted outputs cross a boundary the
adversary controls; for the non-interactive inference schemes here, where the server
never sees a decryption, plain IND-CPA suffices---but RAG deployments that return
decrypted scores or rankings to a queryable client should apply the countermeasure
(\cref{ssec:retr-limits}). The leakage and attacks specific to cryptographic
\emph{retrieval}---distance-comparison inversion, access-pattern and volume
reconstruction---are treated with the retrieval schemes in \cref{sec:retrieval}.
