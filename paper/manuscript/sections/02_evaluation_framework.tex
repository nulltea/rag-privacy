\section{Evaluation Framework: Security and Cost}
\label{sec:framework}

% UNIFIED framework section (grill #5): ONE framing for how we compare schemes, split into
% Security (categorical taxonomy) and Cost (quantitative perf+utility). The categorical/
% quantitative split is stated ONCE here; nothing downstream re-derives it. Side-channel
% finding lives in §\ref{sec:synthesis}.

We compare confidential Transformer-inference and RAG schemes along two kinds of
dimension. \emph{Security} captures categorical properties of who the adversary is and what
stays hidden (\cref{sec:threat}); \emph{cost} is the quantitative price of that
protection, in performance and utility (\cref{sec:comparison}). This single split runs
through the paper: the security dimensions yield the threat-model-fit criterion of our
deployment-readiness lens (\cref{sec:intro}). We also fix here how schemes are
\emph{grouped} for comparison: by use case, not by family (detailed in \cref{sec:comparison}).

\subsection{Taxonomy: Threat Model and Security}
\label{sec:threat}

Confidential Transformer-inference and RAG systems are not built against a single
adversary. Each scheme makes its own assumptions about who the attacker is, what it
knows, what it can observe, and what must stay hidden. Those assumptions, not the
mechanisms alone, decide whether a defense is adequate. We classify the security of each
scheme along five \emph{categorical} axes (\cref{sec:threat-axes}), place
every inference scheme on them (\cref{sec:threat-landscape}), and treat the
attacker's \emph{capability budget} as a cross-cutting concern (\cref{sec:threat-budget}).
A field-wide side-channel blind spot emerges from the exercise (\cref{ssec:sidechannel}).

\subsubsection{Axes}
\label{sec:threat-axes}

We classify each scheme along five axes.

\paragraph{1. Adversary behavior.}
\emph{Honest-but-curious} (HbC): the operator runs the protocol faithfully but inspects every
observable for private information, the standard cloud model, adopted by most schemes here.
\emph{Malicious/active}: the operator may additionally deviate (drop, replay, reorder, tamper),
requiring integrity machinery on top of confidentiality. Behavior decides whether
confidentiality alone suffices.

\paragraph{2. Trust anchor (TCB).}
What the scheme roots its guarantees in: \emph{none} (purely cryptographic, statistical, or
obfuscation-based); a \emph{CPU-TEE} (Intel TDX, AMD SEV-SNP); a \emph{GPU-TEE} (a
confidential H100/H200/B200-class accelerator); or \emph{multi-party non-collusion} (the MPC
assumption). The trusted computing base (TCB) is the set of components that must be trusted not
to leak; a larger TCB means more hardware is trusted, so a purely cryptographic scheme has the
smallest TCB and a GPU-TEE among the largest. A larger TCB trades a stronger trust assumption for
performance or simplicity.
These anchors collapse onto a three-level \emph{hardware-trust} ladder we use to
organize the systematization and its scope (\cref{sec:intro}):
\begin{description}
  \item[Tier 1.] \emph{No trusted hardware}: confidentiality rests on cryptography or
        statistics alone (MPC/FHE, static obfuscation, DP). ``None'' constrains what must be
        \emph{trusted not to leak}, not where the \emph{work} runs. Several Tier-1 schemes are
        not thin-client: client-side private information retrieval or oblivious RAM (PIR/ORAM)
        retrieval, or two-party computation (2PC) where the user is a compute
        party, make the client load-bearing, a deployment factor we revisit in
        \cref{sec:synthesis}.
  \item[Tier 2.] \emph{CPU-TEE only}: a commodity CPU enclave is trusted; any GPU is untrusted.
  \item[Tier 3.] \emph{Confidential accelerator}: a confidential GPU or accelerator-TEE trusted
        end-to-end.
\end{description}
The Tier-2$\to$Tier-3 jump (trusting the accelerator silicon end-to-end) is the sharpest
escalation on this axis, so a scheme's tier is the single most consequential entry in its row;
the hybrid split's defining move (\cref{sec:hybrid}) is to deliver Tier-3-class GPU performance
at Tier~2.

\paragraph{3. Attacker knowledge.}
\emph{Black-box} (the adversary sees only the input/output interface) or \emph{white-box}
(it can additionally inspect the model weights and internal activations, the realistic case
for open-weight Transformers). We adopt Kerckhoffs's principle throughout: in either regime the
adversary knows the algorithm and is denied only a fresh per-session secret, whether an
obfuscation transform, injected DP noise, or cryptographic keys. That secret therefore belongs
to the \emph{security basis} (axis~5), not to a separate knowledge level. White-box is the
regime in which static schemes are most stressed: an obfuscation transform secure only while the
weights are private collapses once an attacker who knows the public weights can solve for it
(\cref{sec:obfuscation}).

\paragraph{4. Confidentiality scope.}
What the scheme keeps hidden and what it leaves exposed are complementary. The protected
\emph{asset} may be the input, the input \emph{and} output, the model weights, or the access
pattern over a corpus. The complement is the \emph{residual leakage}: what the adversary still
observes despite the scheme (obfuscated I/O, mixed activations in untrusted VRAM, bounded
access patterns, or distance structure). Microarchitectural, cache, timing, and power side
channels are a residual category \emph{every} surveyed scheme places out of scope, a
uniformity we elevate to a finding (\cref{ssec:sidechannel}) rather than carry as a per-scheme
cell. Two schemes agreeing on every other axis can diverge sharply here, and it is the residual
leakage, not the stated protection goal, that an attack ultimately exploits.

\paragraph{5. Security basis.}
What the guarantee \emph{rests on}, independent of \emph{which} entity is trusted (axis~2):
\emph{cryptographic} (provable under a hardness assumption, as in FHE/MPC under LWE/OT);
\emph{information-theoretic} (no computational assumption, as in one-time masking);
\emph{differential privacy} (a formal $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ or metric bound, where the privacy
budget $\varepsilon$ caps how much any single record can shift the output and $\delta$ is a small
failure probability);
\emph{hardware-enforced} (reduces to TEE isolation and attestation); and \emph{heuristic}
(resistance shown only experimentally, as in most static obfuscation). This axis separates
schemes the others cannot: an FHE scheme and a static-obfuscation scheme can share an identical
row on axes~1--4 yet differ as cryptographic indistinguishability versus an empirically
measured resistance that can be far weaker than reported. Security basis is thus the sharpest
predictor of whether a confidentiality claim
survives scrutiny, and the axis that \emph{threat-model fit} (\cref{sec:intro}) weights most heavily.

\subsubsection{The Landscape}
\label{sec:threat-landscape}

\cref{tab:threat-taxonomy} places every inference scheme of \cref{tab:inf-perf} on
the five axes. Four patterns stand out. First, the
obfuscation family clusters tightly at \emph{HbC + white-box}: these schemes
explicitly assume the attacker knows the open-source model and the method, and
depend entirely on an ephemeral secret. Second, the trust anchor varies more
than the adversary behavior does: schemes that face nearly the same attacker still
root trust in very different places (nothing, a CPU-TEE, or a GPU-TEE), which is the real
axis of disagreement in the field. Third, defending a \emph{malicious} operator tracks the
trust anchor: the confidential-accelerator designs (Nvidia-CC, Opal, Ascend-CC) withstand a host
that may tamper with computation outside the enclave, and TwinShield an accelerator that may
deviate from the protocol (caught by its U-Verify integrity checks); the cryptographic,
obfuscation, and DP schemes assume an honest-but-curious operator. Fourth, and most consequential, the \emph{security basis} separates the
table where no other axis can: the obfuscation rows rest on heuristic,
experimentally-measured resistance, while the cryptographic
schemes rest on hardness assumptions and the TEE designs on hardware. Several
heuristic and cryptographic schemes alike anchor trust in \emph{nothing}
(trust anchor \emph{none}), so this distinction is invisible without the basis
axis.

\subsubsection{The Attacker's Capability Budget}
\label{sec:threat-budget}

The five axes say what an adversary \emph{is}, not how \emph{much} it can spend. Yet the
capability budget (compute, and especially the observation horizon: a single inference vs.\
many adaptive runs) is decisive, because the surveyed schemes target markedly different
budgets. AloePri is explicit that it ``aims to provide adjustable security for constrained attackers in
real-world scenarios, rather than offering ideal-world security guarantees against worst-case
attackers'' \citep{lin_aloepri}; GELO's
guarantee is per-batch non-identifiability under a fresh mixing matrix, conceding nothing
\emph{across} batches but leaving a single-batch blind-source-separation problem
\citep{belikov_gelo}. A worst-case adversary with unbounded compute and unlimited adaptive
observation defeats every static-obfuscation scheme here; an evaluation assuming one would
measure nothing interesting and misrepresent schemes that never claimed it. This is why
\emph{threat-model fit}, not maximality, is a deployment-readiness criterion: a scheme
defending an adversary stronger than its deployment faces overpays in performance, one
defending a weaker adversary fails to protect, and the deployable point is the calibrated one,
on which we score families in \cref{sec:synthesis}.

We therefore treat the budget as an explicit per-scheme parameter, assessing each scheme
\emph{within its own constrained-attacker regime} (white-box on weights and method, denied the
per-session secret, under the compute and observation horizon the scheme's own evaluation
permits), so that a reported break is a break by the victim's own standard, not an artifact of
an inflated adversary.

\subsection{Cost: Performance and Utility}
\label{sec:comparison}

The other two deployment-readiness criteria are quantitative: \emph{performance}
(overhead and latency) and \emph{utility} (impact on output quality). Comparing them
across papers is hard to do fairly: reported numbers come from different hardware, model
sizes, datasets, and metrics, so a raw side-by-side can mislead. We fix here a common set of
\emph{reference metrics} for both, and state how we group schemes when we compare them.

\paragraph{Normalizing performance.}
Wherever a paper gives a plaintext baseline we express cost as an \emph{overhead multiplier}
($\times$ vs.\ plaintext on the same hardware and model); otherwise we compare only \emph{within
a regime}, a (model-size $\times$ hardware) bucket (e.g.\ BERT-base on one GPU; a 7B decoder
over WAN), never across regimes. Cross-scheme figures are \emph{indicative} by default (each
from its own testbed: an order of magnitude, not a precise ratio); only figures we flag
\emph{co-measured} (benchmarked together in one paper) support direct comparison. Even within a
regime, a comparison is fair only when the number of parties, the numerical precision, and the
network setting match. Chen et al.~\citep{chen_sok_pti} find WAN-tuned schemes miss their
reported numbers under other network profiles, so LAN and WAN figures are not interchangeable.

\paragraph{Normalizing utility.}
Schemes that compute the true function (cryptography, TEE, invertible-mask hybrid splits) are
\emph{exact}; approximate schemes (obfuscation, DP, polynomial-approximation MPC) are reported
as a \emph{delta} against the plaintext baseline in the use-case-appropriate metric, so a
retrieval recall drop and a generation accuracy drop are never conflated.

\paragraph{Grouping: by use case, not by family.}
We \emph{systematize} by mechanism family (\cref{sec:crypto}--\cref{sec:hybrid}) but
\emph{compare} performance and utility \textbf{by use case}, since a deployment decision weighs
every mechanism that serves a stage against the others (private generation pits MPC, TEE,
obfuscation, and hybrid split against each other, not FHE against obfuscation in the abstract).
We use four use cases aligned with the gap-map stages (\cref{sec:synthesis}): \emph{generation}
(decoder) and \emph{embedding} (encoder) inference, and retrieval split by index structure into
\emph{vector} (flat/IVF ANN and vector storage) and \emph{graph} (graph-based ANN and
graph-semantic search), which impose different costs and threat surfaces; each scheme is grouped
by the model class it evaluates. \emph{Reranking} is cross-encoder inference, so it inherits the embedding and generation
schemes (only Opal exercises it directly, in its enclave pipeline); no surveyed work optimizes a
standalone private reranker, which we leave as an open problem. We give it no separate cost
bucket, and the gap-map (\cref{sec:synthesis}) shows it covered by the inference families. Each use case has a
canonical metric: latency and $\times$-overhead for generation and embedding; latency/query and
communication at a fixed corpus size for retrieval; and utility as the task-accuracy
(generation/embedding) or recall@$k$ (retrieval) delta against the plaintext baseline.
