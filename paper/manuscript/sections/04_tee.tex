\section{Trusted Execution Environments}
\label{sec:tee}

% SCOPE ROLE: TEE is the Tier-3 performance/trust BASELINE (HW-gated). Pure-TEE inference
% (full model in a CPU enclave, or on a confidential accelerator) gives near-plaintext
% performance but DEMANDS confidential-compute hardware and full enclave trust. Spine =
% hardware-availability axis: CPU-TEE (Tier 2) -> confidential GPU (Tier 3, baseline) ->
% confidential NPU (parallel substrate). The HW requirement, NOT the performance, motivates
% the Tier-2 commodity-GPU hybrid split (sec:hybrid). Side-channel finding bites hardest here.

A trusted execution environment runs the computation in plaintext inside a
hardware-isolated enclave and proves to the client, by remote attestation, that the expected
code and model are what executes. Pure-TEE inference is our \emph{baseline}: it achieves
near-plaintext speed and shifts trust to the silicon vendor, but at full scale it demands
either a CPU enclave large enough for the model (Tier~2, slow for large models) or a
\emph{confidential accelerator} (Tier~3). That accelerator is now vendor-plural: a
confidential NVIDIA GPU (an H100/B200-class GPU-TEE) or a confidential NPU (the Huawei Ascend
line). The schemes here are therefore predominantly Tier~3, which we keep as the
performance/trust baseline rather than systematize in depth: once the accelerator is trusted
end-to-end, confidentiality reduces to enclave construction and attestation, a
hardware-specific space systematized separately~\citep{accel_tee_sok}. That Tier-3 hardware
requirement, not the performance, is what motivates the Tier-2 commodity-GPU hybrid split
(\cref{sec:hybrid}). The strongest adversary in our corpus appears here: Opal~\cite{kaviani_opal}
assumes a \emph{malicious host} and additionally hides access patterns with ORAM. On the
deployment-readiness criteria, pure TEE scores highest on performance and fidelity, but its
Tier-3 hardware dependence is the binding deployability constraint (\cref{sec:synthesis}).

\subsection{Approaches}
% Bold-name paragraphs grouped by hardware band (the spine). CPU-TEE (Tier 2) ->
% confidential GPU (H100-CC, PipeLLM, Opal) -> confidential NPU (Ascend-CC). PipeLLM and
% TensorTEE re-surface in Performance as the two overhead-reducers.
End-to-end confidentiality is the deployment workflow that binds the hardware guarantee to the
threat model, not the enclave alone~\citep{andreoletti_ppi_practice}. Four steps recur:
\emph{remote attestation}, where the client verifies the enclave's measurement (the expected
model, runtime, and configuration); \emph{key provisioning}, where session keys are released to
the enclave only after attestation succeeds; a \emph{secure channel}, where prompts are
decrypted and processed only inside the enclave and completions are re-encrypted before leaving
it; and \emph{output handling}, where plaintext logging, tracing, and telemetry outside the
boundary are forbidden by policy. In the \cref{sec:framework} taxonomy the trusted core is a
CPU-TEE or a confidential accelerator, the adversary is honest-but-curious by default (Opal the
exception), and the residual surface is what escapes the boundary: token-streaming timing and
length, and the microarchitectural side channels of \cref{ssec:sidechannel}. The schemes differ
in which hardware holds the enclave.

The simplest design runs the entire model inside a \textbf{CPU enclave} (Intel TDX, AMD
SEV-SNP). It needs no special accelerator (Tier~2), but it is bounded by enclave memory
bandwidth and the absence of a GPU, so it is impractical for large models; it is the floor of
the family, not a deployment target.

The confidential-GPU band runs the model on a trusted accelerator. \textbf{The confidential
GPU}~\citep{h100cc} places the whole model in a hardware enclave on an H100/H200/B200-class
accelerator, encrypts its HBM, and protects the CPU--GPU PCIe link with AES-GCM; paired with an
attested CPU confidential VM at the other end, it delivers whole-model confidentiality at
near-plaintext speed, and is the performance and trust baseline the rest of the paper measures
against. \textbf{PipeLLM}~\citep{pipellm} targets the one place a confidential GPU pays, the
encrypted PCIe channel: it speculatively precomputes the AES-GCM keystream so the link
encryption overlaps computation instead of serializing behind it. \textbf{Opal}~\citep{kaviani_opal}
carries the strongest adversary in our corpus, a malicious host that may tamper with computation
outside the enclave, and additionally hides access patterns with oblivious RAM over an encrypted
disk, running an in-enclave knowledge-graph and ANN retrieval pipeline on a TDX host with a B200
accelerator.

The confidential accelerator need not be an NVIDIA GPU. \textbf{Ascend-CC}~\citep{ascend_cc}
makes a discrete NPU (a Huawei Ascend 910A) the \emph{sole} hardware root of trust, with no CPU
TEE and a fully malicious host. Before any plaintext exists, the NPU unmaps its memory from the
host's address space by removing the DMA mappings, then decrypts the data and model inside the
device; results are re-encrypted before that memory is remapped for the host to read. Task and
binary attestation block a malicious host from injecting rogue operators into the execution.
Because it encrypts the model parameters and operator binaries as well as the input data, it
serves three mutually distrusting parties at once (the data owner, the model owner, and the
cloud), and it requires no change to the PyTorch stack. It shows the Tier-3 requirement is
vendor-plural, but it is a requirement still: Ascend-CC depends on an on-chip security module
and hardware root of trust that a commodity device lacks.

\subsection{Performance}
\label{ssec:tee-perf}
Overhead is near-plaintext and single-digit-percent across the band (\cref{tab:inf-perf}): the
confidential GPU adds $4$--$8\%$ on Llama-2 7--70B, and Ascend-CC under $0.1\%$ on Llama-2/3 at
realistic sequence lengths. Both shrink as the model and the sequence grow, because the fixed
trust cost amortizes over more computation; the percentage overheads that look large
(Ascend-CC's $16\%$ at a 50-token input) come only from tiny workloads where the trust machinery
is not amortized.

The residual cost is not the computation but the trust machinery around it, and it has two
sources. The first is the encrypted CPU--accelerator channel: on the current Hopper installed
base, PCIe transfers are staged through a software bounce buffer with AES-GCM, which the
benchmark literature finds dominates the residual overhead rather than the GPU work
itself~\citep{chrapek_gputee}. The second is inside the enclave, where memory protection carries
version-number and message-authentication-code (MAC) metadata and Merkle-tree traversal on every
off-chip access. Two hardware optimizations attack these in turn. \emph{PipeLLM} pipelines the
channel encryption, overlapping the AES-GCM with computation to hold a confidential GPU near
$1.2\times$~\citep{pipellm}. \emph{TensorTEE} attacks the in-enclave metadata: a unified
tensor-granularity for the CPU and NPU enclaves keeps version numbers on-chip, replaces
cacheline MACs with delayed-verification tensor-wise MACs, and moves data across enclaves without
re-encryption, cutting a heterogeneous CPU--NPU TEE to $2.1\%$ over non-secure execution, a
$4.0\times$ speedup over prior heterogeneous designs~\citep{tensortee}. Its evaluation is LLM
training in simulation, but the metadata it removes is incurred by any TEE-protected tensor
workload, inference included.

These optimizations are software workarounds for a cost that hardware is beginning to absorb.
Inline PCIe TEE-IO, the TDISP and IDE standards adopted as Intel TDX-Connect, AMD SEV-TIO, and
NVIDIA Blackwell trusted-I/O, encrypts the link at the protocol level and removes the bounce
buffer entirely, but only when both the CPU and the accelerator support it, which the installed
base does not yet~\citep{nvidia_secureai}. Opal sits above the band: oblivious RAM hides access
patterns at an extra cost, though its in-enclave retrieval still reports $29\times$ lower
infrastructure than a comparable secure baseline~\citep{kaviani_opal}. Fidelity is exact
throughout, since a TEE computes the true function in plaintext.

The attacks here split by whether a scheme can close them, and the line between the two kinds is
the section's main caveat (\cref{tab:tee-attacks}). The structural kind is not closed by anything
in this family. A TEE anchors its entire trust in silicon whose best-documented weakness is
exactly microarchitectural and physical leakage, and every scheme in the band, TensorTEE and
Ascend-CC included, places side channels out of scope. WeSee breaks AMD SEV-SNP through
hypervisor-injected interrupts~\citep{wesee}; TEE.Fail extracts keys and forges attestation with
a sub-\$1000 DDR5 bus interposer across Intel SGX/TDX, AMD SEV-SNP, and confidential-mode
GPUs~\citep{tee_fail}; LeftoverLocals reconstructs responses from leaked GPU local
memory~\citep{leftoverlocals}. None is defended by the schemes here. We record this as the
field-wide blind spot of \cref{ssec:sidechannel}, which falls hardest on exactly the
hardware-anchored families because their whole guarantee is the silicon.

The same anchoring places trust in the silicon vendor (Intel, AMD, NVIDIA, or Huawei).
Attestation verifies that the right code runs, not that the hardware itself is correct, so a flaw
in the root of trust undermines the whole chain. TEE.Fail's attestation forgery is the concrete
case: once the bus encryption is broken, the attestation a client relies on can be manufactured.
The deployment-addressable kind is more tractable. Attestation is only as strong as its
configuration, so a deployment must pin the expected measurement and demand fresh quotes; access
patterns leak which corpus items a query touches, which Opal's ORAM answers in-band at a cost;
and some confidential-GPU parts still leave HBM regions unencrypted, a coverage gap closed on
current-generation hardware.

\begin{keyinsight}
On the right hardware, near-plaintext confidential inference is a solved capability: a
confidential GPU runs the whole model at $4$--$8\%$ overhead, and an NPU root of trust reaches
under $0.1\%$ while resisting a malicious host. The capability is gated on possessing a
confidential accelerator, now vendor-plural (an NVIDIA GPU or a Huawei NPU) rather than a
single-vendor lock, but gated all the same. Its one residual risk is uniform across the band and
independent of the scheme: the microarchitectural side-channel blind spot (\cref{ssec:sidechannel})
that the field's threat models scope out, and that falls hardest on designs whose entire trust is
the silicon.
\end{keyinsight}
