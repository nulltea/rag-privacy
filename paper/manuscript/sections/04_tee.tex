\section{Trusted Execution Environments}
\label{sec:tee}

% SCOPE ROLE: TEE is the Tier-3 performance/trust BASELINE (HW-gated). Pure-TEE inference
% (full model in a CPU enclave, or on a confidential accelerator) gives near-plaintext
% performance but DEMANDS confidential-compute hardware and full enclave trust. Spine =
% hardware-availability axis: CPU-TEE (Tier 2) -> confidential GPU (Tier 3, baseline) ->
% confidential NPU (parallel substrate). The HW requirement, NOT the performance, motivates
% the Tier-2 commodity-GPU hybrid split (sec:hybrid). Side-channel finding bites hardest here.

A trusted execution environment runs the computation in plaintext inside a
hardware-isolated enclave and proves to the client, by remote attestation, that the expected
code and model are what executes. It achieves near-plaintext speed and shifts trust to the
silicon vendor, but at full scale it demands either a CPU enclave large enough for the model
(Tier~2, impractical for large models) or a \emph{confidential accelerator} (Tier~3). We cover
two such accelerators: a confidential NVIDIA GPU (an H100/B200-class GPU-TEE) and a confidential
NPU (the Huawei Ascend line). The schemes here are therefore predominantly Tier~3, which we
cover rather than systematize in depth: once the accelerator is trusted end-to-end,
confidentiality reduces to enclave provisioning and attestation, a hardware-specific space
systematized separately~\citep{accel_tee_sok}. The hardware requirement, rather than any
performance gap, is what motivates the Tier-2 commodity-GPU hybrid split (\cref{sec:hybrid}).
Two schemes here assume the corpus's strongest adversary, a \emph{malicious host} that may
tamper with computation outside the enclave: Opal~\citep{kaviani_opal}, which additionally hides
access patterns with ORAM, and Ascend-CC~\citep{ascend_cc}. Pure TEE attains the highest
performance and fidelity in the survey, but its Tier-3 hardware dependence is the binding
deployability constraint; we rank it against the other families in \cref{sec:synthesis}.

\subsection{Approaches}
% Bold-name paragraphs grouped by hardware band (the spine). CPU-TEE (Tier 2) ->
% confidential GPU (H100-CC, PipeLLM, Opal) -> confidential NPU (Ascend-CC). PipeLLM and
% TensorTEE re-surface in Performance as the two overhead-reducers.
End-to-end confidentiality is the deployment workflow that binds the hardware guarantee to the
threat model, not the enclave alone~\citep{andreoletti_ppi_practice}. The workflow has four
parts: \emph{remote attestation}, where the client verifies the enclave's measurement (the hash
of the loaded code, model, and configuration); \emph{key provisioning}, where session keys are
released to the enclave only after attestation succeeds; a \emph{secure channel}, where prompts
are decrypted and processed only inside the enclave and completions are re-encrypted before
leaving it; and \emph{operational discipline}, where plaintext logging, tracing, and telemetry
outside the boundary are forbidden by policy rather than enforced by the hardware. In the
\cref{sec:framework} taxonomy the trusted core is a CPU-TEE or a confidential accelerator, the
adversary is honest-but-curious by default (Opal and Ascend-CC are the exceptions), and the residual
surface is what escapes the boundary: token-streaming timing and length, and the
microarchitectural side channels of \cref{ssec:sidechannel}. The schemes differ in which
hardware holds the enclave.

The simplest design runs the entire model inside a \textbf{CPU enclave} (Intel TDX, AMD
SEV-SNP). It needs no special accelerator (Tier~2), but it is bounded by the enclave's memory
capacity, which caps the model size that fits, and by the absence of a GPU, which throttles
throughput, so it is impractical for large models; it is the floor of the family, not a
deployment target.

The Tier-3 schemes run on a trusted accelerator. \textbf{The confidential GPU}~\citep{h100cc}
places the whole model in a hardware enclave on an H100/H200/B200-class accelerator, encrypts
its HBM, and protects the CPU--GPU PCIe link with AES-GCM; paired with an attested CPU
confidential VM at the other end, it delivers whole-model confidentiality at near-plaintext
speed. \textbf{PipeLLM}~\citep{pipellm} targets the one place a confidential GPU pays, the
encrypted PCIe channel: it speculatively precomputes the AES-GCM keystream so the link
encryption overlaps computation instead of serializing behind it. \textbf{Opal}~\citep{kaviani_opal}
confronts that malicious host directly. Encryption alone does not stop it, since encryption hides
content but not the host's control of scheduling, memory mapping, and message replay; Opal
therefore verifies the integrity and freshness of every result against attested,
Merkle-authenticated state, rejecting any tampered or rolled-back execution. It additionally hides
access patterns with oblivious RAM over an encrypted disk, running an in-enclave knowledge-graph
and ANN retrieval pipeline on a TDX host with a B200 accelerator.

\textbf{Ascend-CC}~\citep{ascend_cc} places the enclave on a discrete NPU instead. It makes a
Huawei Ascend 910A the \emph{sole} hardware root of trust, with no CPU TEE and a fully malicious
host. Before any plaintext exists, the NPU unmaps its memory from the host's address space by
removing the DMA mappings, decrypts the data and model inside the device, and re-encrypts results
before that memory is remapped for the host to read. It attests each operator binary before
execution, so a malicious host cannot substitute a rogue operator that would exfiltrate the
plaintext. Because it encrypts the model parameters and operator binaries as well as the input
data, a single deployment can protect three mutually distrusting parties at once (the data owner,
the model owner, and the cloud), and it requires no change to the PyTorch stack. The confidential
accelerator is thus not limited to one vendor, but it remains a requirement: Ascend-CC depends on
an on-chip security module and hardware root of trust that a commodity device lacks.

\subsection{Performance}
\label{ssec:tee-perf}
Overhead is near-plaintext and single-digit-percent across the band (\cref{tab:inf-perf}): the
confidential GPU adds $4$--$8\%$ on Llama-2 7--70B, and Ascend-CC adds under $0.1\%$ on Llama-2/3
at realistic sequence lengths. These percentages are each platform's own overhead and are not
comparable across hardware classes, because they sit on very different absolute baselines. An
H100-class confidential GPU delivers on the order of $1000$\,TFLOPS of dense FP16 over
$3.35$\,TB/s of HBM3, whereas the Ascend 910A is an earlier 7\,nm NPU at roughly $256$\,TFLOPS
FP16 with markedly lower memory bandwidth, so its near-zero \emph{relative} overhead is measured
against a lower absolute throughput, not evidence that it runs faster. Within a platform the
overhead falls as the model and the sequence grow, because it is a fixed per-request charge
(channel encryption and a one-time setup) that does not scale with the token count and so becomes
a smaller fraction of a larger computation. The charge is visible only on very small models at
very short inputs; on Llama-2/3 it stays below $0.1\%$ across sequence lengths.

The residual cost is not the computation but the trust mechanisms around it, and it has two
sources. The first is the encrypted CPU--accelerator channel. On today's deployed
Hopper-generation GPUs, PCIe transfers are staged through a software bounce buffer that the CPU
encrypts with AES-GCM, and the benchmark literature finds this staging, not the GPU computation,
to be the dominant source of the residual overhead~\citep{chrapek_gputee}. The second source is
inside the enclave, where memory protection attaches version-number and
message-authentication-code (MAC) metadata to each block and walks a Merkle tree on every
off-chip access. Two hardware optimizations attack these in turn. \emph{PipeLLM} pipelines the
channel encryption, overlapping the AES-GCM with computation to hold a confidential GPU near
$1.2\times$ the unprotected latency~\citep{pipellm}. \emph{TensorTEE} attacks the in-enclave
metadata: a unified tensor-granularity for the CPU and NPU enclaves keeps version numbers
on-chip, replaces per-cacheline MACs with delayed-verification tensor-wise MACs, and moves data
between enclaves without re-encryption, which brings a heterogeneous CPU--NPU TEE to within
$2.1\%$ of non-secure execution, a $4.0\times$ speedup over prior heterogeneous
designs~\citep{tensortee}. TensorTEE is evaluated on training workloads in architectural
simulation rather than on deployed silicon, but the metadata it removes is incurred by any
TEE-protected tensor workload, inference included.

These optimizations are software workarounds for a cost that hardware is beginning to absorb.
Inline PCIe TEE-IO, the TDISP and IDE standards adopted as Intel TDX-Connect, AMD SEV-TIO, and
NVIDIA Blackwell trusted-I/O, encrypts the link in the PCIe controller itself and removes the
software bounce buffer entirely, but only when both the CPU and the accelerator support it, which
most deployed systems do not yet provide~\citep{nvidia_secureai}. Opal is the exception here: its
oblivious RAM adds access-pattern hiding at a real cost, $1.57\times$ query latency over a
non-oblivious enclave, but its $O(\log N)$ accesses make oblivious retrieval roughly $29\times$
higher in query throughput, and about an order of magnitude cheaper at scale, than the alternative
of scanning the whole encrypted store in-enclave for the same privacy~\citep{kaviani_opal}. Fidelity
is exact throughout, since a TEE computes the true function in plaintext.

The attacks here split by whether the hardware vendor treats them as in scope, and that line is
the section's main caveat (\cref{tab:tee-attacks}). Most are actively engineered against and
already mitigated. WeSee's malicious-interrupt injection into AMD SEV-SNP is closed by firmware
mitigations~\citep{wesee}, and LeftoverLocals, which reconstructed responses from leaked GPU local
memory, was patched by the affected vendors within months of disclosure~\citep{leftoverlocals}.
These are the normal patch cycle of a maturing platform, not standing holes, and they apply to
the hardware itself, so TensorTEE and Ascend-CC inherit the same exposure and the same fixes.

One class, though, the vendors decline to close. Server-grade TEEs dropped the Merkle-tree
integrity and replay protection of client SGX in favor of deterministic AES-XTS memory
encryption, a deliberate trade-off to support large confidential VMs at low latency. TEE.Fail
exploits exactly that determinism: a sub-\$1000 DDR5 bus interposer reads and replays memory
traffic to extract keys and forge attestation across Intel SGX/TDX, AMD SEV-SNP, and
confidential-mode GPUs, and both AMD and Intel classify such physical attacks as out of scope with
no mitigation planned~\citep{tee_fail}. This is the structural remainder: not an oversight the
field has missed, but a class its threat model deliberately excludes, sitting precisely on the
silicon the whole guarantee rests on. That exclusion is the long-term cost of anchoring trust in
hardware. A guarantee that reduces to ``the silicon is correct'' holds only within the vendor's
threat model, and only until the next attack on the microarchitecture or the memory bus. Such
attacks recur because each generation's performance optimizations (deterministic encryption,
speculation, shared caches, and interconnects) reopen an observable channel. Unlike a
cryptographic assumption, which a scheme can strengthen on its own, a hardware-anchored design
cannot close this without the vendor, and the vendor optimizes for performance first. We treat side channels as out of scope for this
survey too, and current confidential-compute hardware materially raises the bar against
software-only attackers; but we record the exclusion as the finding of \cref{ssec:sidechannel},
because it falls hardest on exactly the TEE and hybrid families whose entire guarantee is the
silicon.

The deployment-addressable gaps are more tractable. Attestation is only as strong as its
configuration, so a deployment must pin the expected measurement and require a fresh, nonce-bound
quote on each session; otherwise an attacker can replay a stale attestation, or, as TEE.Fail
shows, forge one outright once the bus encryption is broken. Access patterns leak which corpus
items a query touches, which Opal's ORAM answers in-band at a cost, and some confidential-GPU
parts still leave HBM regions unencrypted, a coverage gap closed on current-generation hardware.

\begin{keyinsight}
On the right hardware, near-plaintext confidential inference is a solved capability: a
confidential GPU runs the whole model at $4$--$8\%$ overhead, and an NPU root of trust reaches
under $0.1\%$ while resisting a malicious host. The capability is gated on possessing a
confidential accelerator, which more than one vendor now supplies but which a commodity device
still lacks. Its one residual risk is the same for every design in the family and independent of
the mechanism: a physical or microarchitectural attack on the silicon the guarantee rests on, a
class the vendor threat model deliberately excludes (\cref{ssec:sidechannel}) and that no design
here can close on its own.
\end{keyinsight}
