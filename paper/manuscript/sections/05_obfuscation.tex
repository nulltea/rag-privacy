\section{Static Obfuscation}
\label{sec:obfuscation}

% --- scope-framing (short role-framing) ---
Static obfuscation transforms the data and/or weights so the server sees only
values it cannot interpret, with no---or minimal---trusted hardware. Obfuscation is the cheapest family in our taxonomy (near-plaintext cost, no trusted
hardware---Tier~1), which is precisely why its privacy claims deserve the most scrutiny:
the obfuscation is \emph{fixed}, so a white-box adversary can analyze it in advance. We make
that scrutiny concrete in \cref{app:validation}, and the published inversions in
\cref{sec:synthesis} show several static schemes recover far more plaintext than
their authors report. On the deployment-readiness criteria, obfuscation leads on performance
and fidelity, but \emph{threat-model fit} is its weak axis. A second deployment advantage is
infrastructural: because the model runs unmodified, static obfuscation slots into production
inference stacks (vLLM, llama.cpp) directly---unlike crypto or TEE schemes, which require a
bespoke serving path. The Tier-1 label is nonetheless qualified for the schemes that
obfuscate the \emph{weights}: someone trusted must generate and hold the secret, and sharing
the resulting obfuscated model across tenants likely forces that step into a TEE---whereas
the input-transform schemes keep a stock, shareable model and a client-side secret. We return
to this split in \cref{ssec:deployment}.

\subsection{Approaches}
Every scheme in this family makes the same design choice: a client-side transform of the
input (and, in all the schemes we survey, a matching transform of the weights) so that the
server runs a stock inference graph on values it cannot interpret and returns results the
client de-obfuscates. In the \cref{sec:framework} taxonomy the trusted core is none or
light (Tier~1, at most a TEE holding keys), the adversary is an honest-but-curious,
white-box operator, and the observation surface is the obfuscated input/output (and, for
KV-cache schemes, the externalized cache). The schemes differ in \emph{what} they transform
and \emph{how} the hiding randomness is structured.

\textbf{ObfusLM}~\citep{obfuslm} permutes and re-synthesizes the vocabulary, embedding, and
head under a \emph{provable} $(k,\varepsilon)$-anonymity guarantee (cluster size $k$, DP
budget $\varepsilon$)---a formal footing rare in this otherwise heuristic family---and,
because the vocabulary itself is permuted, protects the \emph{generated output} as well as
the input, at the cost of fine-tuning the model on the obfuscated embeddings before serving.
\textbf{AloePri}~\citep{lin_aloepri}, its covariant successor, carries the secret token
permutation beyond the embedding and head into the \emph{remaining} weights via invertible
matrices plus Gaussian noise, so the server computes blindly on a stock-shaped graph with no
fine-tuning; the client likewise decodes the permuted response, so it too protects the output. Its hiding strength is set by a keymat half-expansion $h$ that
widens the residual stream to $d_{\text{obs}}{=}d{+}2h$: larger $h$ buys more heuristic
hiding but raises the transform's condition number (compounding through depth) and the
compute width, the three-way knob that makes its trade-off concrete.
\textbf{SGT}~\citep{roberts_sgt} (Stained Glass) applies an
offline-trained, input-conditioned affine/Gaussian transform to the embedding sequence,
optimized under a mutual-information loss; the model runs unmodified on the transformed
embeddings. \textbf{OSNIP}~\citep{cao_osnip} projects the input embedding onto an obfuscated
semantic null space (near-orthogonal to the recoverable subspace) via a client-side
encryptor network trained against the server LLM. \textbf{Eguard}~\citep{eguard} trains a
projection network with a dual-level mutual-information objective explicitly against
Vec2Text inversion, driving inversion $F_1$ to ${\approx}4\%$ while keeping ${>}98\%$ task
accuracy. \textbf{KV-Cloak}~\citep{luo_shadowcache} obfuscates the KV-cache as
$K'{=}S\,\hat{P}\,(K{+}A)\,M$---persistent invertible $S,M$ fused offline into the attention
weights, a one-time permutation $\hat{P}$ refreshed \emph{per PagedAttention block}, and an
additive beacon mask $A$ enabling stateless permutation recovery---with the secret matrices
held in a TEE for key custody only (no compute is offloaded to the enclave); it adds
$<$0.45\% to prefill and is lossless.

Output- and domain-specific variants extend the family. Equivariant Encryption
(\textbf{EE})~\citep{buban_ee} swaps each layer's operators for transformed equivalents that preserve
the linear maps and a fixed set of non-linearities at near-zero overhead, but offers no
formal security argument beyond the size of its key space---a commercial proposal we include
with that caveat. \textbf{CodeCipher}~\citep{codecipher} targets the source-code domain: a
token-to-token confusion map (learned on the embedding matrix) that disguises code sent to a
cloud coding assistant while preserving the model's task output (completion, summarization,
translation).

\subsection{Performance and Accuracy}
\label{sec:obfusc-perf}
Read off the headline columns of \cref{tab:inf-perf}, this family looks unambiguously
cheap: every scheme runs at ${\approx}1\times$ plaintext and reports near-lossless fidelity.
Both numbers, however, are \emph{sweet-spot} readings of a control knob that trades
security, accuracy, and performance against one another simultaneously
(\cref{tab:obfusc-tradeoffs}); the nuance the headline hides is where each scheme pays.

\emph{Performance.} Online cost is genuinely small---KV-Cloak adds $<$0.45\% to prefill, EE
and AloePri are near-zero, the learned transforms add milliseconds at the client, and only
Eguard pays a visible ${\approx}1.7\times$ by running its projection at inference. The
deployment-relevant cost is therefore \emph{offline}, and it splits the family in two. The
covariant matrix schemes (AloePri, KV-Cloak) and EE need only a one-time weight transform,
computed in minutes with no training; the learned transforms pay a real training bill---each
trains a small add-on network on top of the frozen pretrained model, not the model itself.
SGT trains an input-conditioned transform (hours to days), OSNIP trains a client-side
encryptor, and Eguard inserts a $24$-layer encoder between the embedding and the secured
space---making each training step $1.6$--$3.4\times$ slower than the same step without the
defense, the extra cost being that encoder's forward/backward pass and its
mutual-information objective. ObfusLM is the heaviest: it additionally fine-tunes the model
itself on the obfuscated embeddings (server-side hours) before it can serve, tying the
obfuscation to one checkpoint. The headline ``${\approx}0$ inference overhead'' thus conceals a setup
cost for half the family. Set against this, the infrastructural advantage is genuine: because
the served model is a stock checkpoint, AloePri and KV-Cloak drop into vLLM/SGLang and inherit
KV-cache and paged-attention optimizations that crypto and TEE serving paths cannot.

\emph{Accuracy.} Fidelity is not free either, and it is the hiding knob that sets it. KV-Cloak
and EE are exactly lossless (their transforms are algebraic identities), so accuracy is
decoupled from their security parameter. The rest face a privacy--utility curve: ObfusLM's
$(k,\varepsilon)$-anonymity loses ${\approx}4\%$ (classification) to ${\approx}6\%$
(generation) and degrades monotonically as the anonymity set $k$ grows; Eguard holds
${>}98\%$ task accuracy only because its objective is tuned against inversion specifically;
and AloePri is the sharpest case---its keymat expansion $h$ widens the residual stream to
$d_{\text{obs}}{=}d{+}2h$ for more hiding, but the transform's condition number compounds
through the model's depth, so accuracy falls off a \emph{cliff} past a model-dependent $h$
(and its RoPE-window parameter $\beta$ is a discrete pass/fail rather than a smooth dial).
AloePri's reported $0$--$3.5\%$ loss is the sweet-spot operating point, not a guarantee that
holds as the knob is turned for stronger hiding. The lesson for deployment is that the three
axes are coupled: a scheme reporting ${\approx}1\times$ and near-lossless has typically
\emph{spent its budget on the security axis}, which is where the published attacks
(\cref{sec:obfusc-attacks}) collect the bill.

\subsection{Limitations and Known Attacks}
\label{sec:obfusc-attacks}
Published attacks substantiate the field-level claim that static obfuscation
\emph{under-protects} relative to its reported guarantees. We group them by the schemes they
break (\cref{tab:obfusc-attacks}); the hybrid-split-specific attacks on offloaded weights and
activations (Game of Arrows, GELO's Gram/ICA leakage, static-basis recovery) are deferred to
\cref{sec:hybrid}.

\emph{Embedding and nearest-neighbour inversion.} Learned embedding transforms are the
softest target. The inversion attack on obfuscated embedding matrices~\citep{lin_inversion}
recovers input tokens from glide-reflection-style transforms, and nearest-neighbour
embedding-inversion attacks~\citep{kornaropoulos_knn_2019,naveed_ppe_2015} match obfuscated
vectors back to a public embedding table; AloePri's own evaluation reports several
state-of-the-art embedding obfuscations losing $>$30\% accuracy while still leaking $>$50\%
of tokens. The defenses are inversion-hardened training (Eguard), a null-space projection
(OSNIP), or a large anonymity set (ObfusLM's $k$).

\emph{Static-permutation collapse.} When the secret is a fixed permutation and the weights
are public, the adversary can solve for it: schemes whose hiding is a static permutation or
key collapse once an attacker downloads the open weights. Hidden-No-More~\citep{hnm}
reconstructs prompts near-perfectly from the hidden states of permutation+noise schemes
(STIP~\citep{stip}, Centaur~\citep{centaur}), and the KV-cache Collision
attack~\citep{luo_shadowcache} defeats KV-Shield's~\citep{kvshield} fixed shuffle because the
shuffle preserves the statistical distribution the attack aligns against. Covariant schemes
are not automatically safe---AloePri's per-head permutations are themselves probed by
weight-fingerprinting that matches public-weight SVD spectra~\citep{lin_aloepri} (recovery
rates are a companion result, \needswork)---which is why noise and non-reused mixing matter.

\emph{KV-cache reconstruction.} Externalizing the KV-cache opens three
attacks~\citep{luo_shadowcache}: \emph{Inversion} reconstructs the input directly from cached
keys/values, \emph{Collision} exploits the algebraic weight--cache correspondence (the one
that breaks KV-Shield), and \emph{Injection} parses the cache with a pristine model. KV-Cloak
answers all three with a per-block one-time permutation plus a beacon mask.

\emph{Deeper-layer false privacy.} The intuition that obfuscating only deep-layer
representations is safe is itself attackable: inversion succeeds at depth~\citep{depth_false_privacy},
so leaving early layers unprotected is not sufficient.

The throughline across these results is a single design lesson: the schemes that survive are
the ones whose hiding randomness is \emph{dynamic and never reused}---KV-Cloak's per-block
one-time permutation and AloePri's covariant transform on the static side, GELO's per-batch
re-mixing on the hybrid side (\cref{sec:hybrid}). The broken schemes (STIP, Centaur, KV-Shield)
all reuse a static permutation or key, which is what lets a white-box adversary average across
queries and solve for the secret.
