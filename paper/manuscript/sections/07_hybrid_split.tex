\section{Hybrid Split: TEE + Obfuscation}
\label{sec:hybrid}

Among the surveyed families, the hybrid split offers the most balanced
deployment-readiness profile (\cref{sec:intro})---a ranking we substantiate in
\cref{sec:synthesis}, not assume here. It keeps a small trusted core in a CPU-TEE
(SGX/TDX, ubiquitous) and offloads the heavy linear algebra to an \emph{untrusted
commodity GPU} over obfuscated activations---no confidential GPU required---combining
near-baseline performance with exact fidelity at Tier-2 trust. It thus sits between
pure-TEE (\cref{sec:tee}: near-plaintext, but needs a confidential GPU at scale) and
cryptography (\cref{sec:crypto}: no special hardware, but $100$--$1000\times$). We
cover the modern practical schemes below; the CNN-era roots
(Slalom~\citep{tramer_slalom}, DarKnight~\citep{hashemi_darknight},
ShadowNet~\citep{sun_shadownet}, SOTER~\citep{shen_soter}) are lineage only, and
TEESlice~\citep{zhang_teeslice} is the analysis showing why a \emph{naive},
un-obfuscated split leaks under public weights.

\subsection{Approach}
% Keep a small trusted core in a TEE; offload the heavy linear algebra to an
% untrusted accelerator over OBFUSCATED activations. Combines TEE's near-plaintext
% speed on the bulk with obfuscation's cheapness, bounded by what the accelerator
% can infer from mixed activations. Map onto §2: TCB = GPU-TEE (small); HbC +
% white-box; observation surface = accelerator activations.
Every scheme in this family makes the same two-part design choice: \emph{what} to keep
in a hardware enclave, and \emph{how} to hide the rest. A small trusted core---a
CPU-TEE (SGX/TDX) or a confidential GPU---holds the secrets and runs the operations
that are cheap but disclosive: a secret transform and its inverse, the non-linearities,
or merely the obfuscation keys. The bulk of the cost, the dense linear algebra of the
QKV and output projections and the feed-forward GEMMs, is offloaded to an
\emph{untrusted, commodity} accelerator that never sees plaintext. Correctness is
recovered exactly inside the TEE, so unlike DP (\cref{sec:dp}) the family pays no
fidelity tax. In the \cref{sec:framework} taxonomy the TCB is the enclave alone (small),
the adversary is an honest-but-curious, white-box operator, and the observation surface
is the stream of obfuscated activations crossing the PCIe boundary.

The schemes differ only in those two knobs. GELO~\citep{belikov_gelo} mixes each batch's
hidden states by a \emph{fresh} secret invertible matrix $A$ ($U{=}AH$), offloads the
projections on $U$, and unmixes by $A^{-1}$ in the TEE; security rests on the
single-batch blind-source-separation hardness of recovering $H$ from $U$.
TwinShield~\citep{xue_twinshield} secret-shares \emph{both} linear and non-linear layers
across a CPU-TEE root and the accelerator, adding U-Verify integrity checks (a Freivalds
generalization, the first to verify even the non-linear SoftMax) so the accelerator may
be \emph{malicious}. ObfuscaTune~\citep{frikha_obfuscatune} keeps only ${\sim}5\%$ of the
parameters in the TEE and offloads obfuscated activations and weights under a one-time
obfuscation matrix. SCX~\citep{zeng_scx} encodes the KV-cache statelessly under a
per-session one-time key ($(\varepsilon,0)$-DP). Portcullis~\citep{portcullis} narrows the
trusted core to a TDX-attested NER gateway that strips PII before any text reaches a
third-party LLM. The common thread---and the family's security argument---is that the
hiding randomness is \emph{dynamic and never reused}, not that the hardware conceals the
GPU: this is precisely what the static-partition lineage lacked
(\cref{sec:hybrid}, Limitations).

\subsection{Representative Schemes}
The hybrid-split \emph{inference} schemes---GELO (per-batch invertible mixing $U{=}AH$, QKV/O
offloaded), TwinShield (CPU-TEE root + secret sharing for linear \emph{and} non-linear, with
U-Verify integrity), ObfuscaTune ($\sim$5\% of parameters kept in the TEE), SCX (per-session
one-time-key KV-cache, $(\varepsilon,0)$-DP), and Portcullis (TEE-attested PII-anonymization
gateway)---are reported in the shared inference table (\cref{tab:inf-perf}). On-device
model-protection splits (SecureInfer~\cite{secureinfer}, Amulet~\cite{amulet}) defend the
\emph{model} from the device owner and are out of scope (\cref{sec:intro}).
The graph-RAG anonymization scheme PrivGemo (dual-tower KG with HMAC session identifiers)
applies the same anonymization lineage to the \emph{retrieval} layer; it is covered with the
other graph-retrieval schemes in \cref{sec:retrieval}.
\dnote{This family is the substrate the COMPANION paper builds on (GELO
optimizations + a unified static+dynamic design). Here we survey it only.}

\subsection{Performance (Reported)}
The reported numbers (\cref{tab:inf-perf}) trace a single curve: the more linear algebra
a scheme can safely offload, the closer it runs to plaintext. GELO offloads
${\approx}76\%$ of the linear-algebra cost of a Llama-2-7B configuration at a
$20$--$30\%$ overhead over \emph{insecure} offloading ($1.2$--$1.3\times$ end-to-end);
TwinShield pushes the offloaded fraction to $87\%$, trading a $5.4\times$ slowdown
versus TEE-only for the addition of verifiable integrity (still $4.0$--$6.1\times$
faster than prior verifiable inference). At the cheap end, ObfuscaTune reports
$1.5$--$4.3\times$ over unprotected inference, while SCX and Portcullis are effectively
free online ($\sim$$1\times$ and $\sim$$1.01\times$), having moved their one-time key or
matrix setup offline. Every scheme preserves exact (or near-exact, modulo
conditioning) fidelity.

This profile is why the hybrid split is the practical frontier for confidential
inference: it needs only a CPU-TEE---SGX/TDX, already shipping in commodity
servers---plus ordinary untrusted GPUs, where pure-TEE inference at scale demands a
\emph{confidential} accelerator (\cref{sec:tee}) and cryptography pays
$100$--$1000\times$ (\cref{sec:crypto}). The family thus occupies a Tier-2 niche:
near-baseline latency and exact fidelity without specialized confidential-compute
hardware. GELO is the partial exception, anchoring its trusted core in a confidential
H200 while still offloading to commodity L40S GPUs.

\subsection{Limitations and Known Attacks}
% GELO names its own leakage: Gram-matrix invariants under orthogonal mixing
% (U^T U = H^T H), mitigated by non-orthogonal A or shield vectors. Security is
% single-batch only.
Three limitations recur across the family. \emph{First, the obfuscation leaks
second-order structure.} GELO names its own channel: under an orthogonal mixing $A$ the
Gram matrix is invariant ($U^{\top}U{=}H^{\top}H$), exposing pairwise inner products of
the hidden states; the mitigation is a non-orthogonal $A$ or appended shield vectors,
and the guarantee holds only \emph{per batch}---freshness is load-bearing, and key reuse
collapses it~\citep{belikov_gelo}.

\emph{Second, the partition is not the protection.} The static-partition lineage is a
cautionary tale: TEESlice~\citep{zhang_teeslice} shows that a naive TEE-shielded
partition falls to model-stealing and membership-inference once the public part is
observable; SOTER's static parameter-morphing basis is fully broken---confidentiality
and integrity---by reuse cryptanalysis that recovers a LLaMA-3-8B layer in
${\sim}6$~minutes~\citep{saini_precomp_break}; and KV-Shield's fixed weight permutation
$W_P{=}WR$ is solvable under public weights and falls to the KV-cache Collision Attack
(\cref{sec:obfuscation})~\citep{kvshield}. The surviving schemes earn their security from
dynamic, never-reused randomness (GELO's per-batch mixing, SCX's per-session key), not
from the split itself.

\emph{Third, the trust and hardware assumptions are real.} Security is honest-but-curious
and white-box by default---only TwinShield's U-Verify extends to a malicious
accelerator---and the co-located TEE-plus-GPU design carries the cost of encrypting the
activation stream across PCIe, with residual exposure from unencrypted HBM on some
confidential-GPU parts. The externalized KV-cache that this family deliberately leaves
outside the enclave for throughput is itself a reconstruction surface, attacked and
defended in \cref{sec:obfuscation}.
