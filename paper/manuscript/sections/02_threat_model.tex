\section{Threat Models in Confidential LLM/RAG: A Taxonomy}
\label{sec:threat}

Confidential LLM and RAG systems are not built against a single adversary.
Each scheme makes its own assumptions about who the attacker is, what it
knows, what it can observe, and what must stay hidden---and those assumptions,
not the mechanisms alone, decide whether a defence is adequate. This section
systematises that design space. We define six axes
(\cref{sec:threat-axes}), place a representative set of schemes on them
(\cref{sec:threat-landscape}), treat the attacker's \emph{capability budget}
as a cross-cutting concern (\cref{sec:threat-budget}), and surface one
field-wide blind spot (\cref{ssec:sidechannel}). Our own measurement adversary
is a single point in this space; we defer it to the evaluation setup
(\cref{sec:eval}).

\subsection{Axes}
\label{sec:threat-axes}

We classify each scheme along six axes.

\paragraph{1. Adversary intent.}
\emph{Honest-but-curious} (HbC): the operator runs the protocol faithfully but
mines every observable for private information. \emph{Malicious/active}: the
operator may additionally deviate---drop, replay, reorder, or tamper with
computation. The obfuscation and TEE-split schemes we study---AloePri
\citep{lin_aloepri}, GELO \citep{belikov_gelo}, TwinShield
\citep{xue_twinshield}, ObfuscaTune \citep{frikha_obfuscatune}---adopt the HbC
model standard in cloud settings. Opal \citep{kaviani_opal} is the outlier: it
defends a malicious host that may tamper with everything outside the enclave.
Intent decides whether confidentiality alone suffices or integrity machinery
is also required.

\paragraph{2. Trust anchor (TCB).}
What the scheme roots its guarantees in. We distinguish: \emph{none} (purely
cryptographic or obfuscation-based---AloePri assumes no trusted hardware); a
\emph{CPU-TEE} (Intel SGX/TDX, e.g.\ TwinShield's root of trust); a
\emph{GPU-TEE} (a confidential H100/H200/B200-class accelerator, e.g.\ GELO's
trusted device); and \emph{multi-party non-collusion} (the assumption
underlying MPC schemes such as PermLLM \citep{permllm}). A larger TCB buys
performance or simplicity at the price of a stronger trust assumption.

\paragraph{3. Attacker knowledge.}
\emph{Black-box} (queries only), \emph{white-box on weights} (knows the public
model---the realistic case for open-source LLMs), or \emph{white-box plus the
obfuscation scheme} (knows the algorithm, ignorant only of an ephemeral
per-session secret). We adopt Kerckhoffs's principle throughout: across the
corpus the adversary knows the model and the method and is denied only the
fresh secret---AloePri's token permutation, GELO's per-batch mixing matrix.
This is the honest assumption for open-weight deployments, and precisely the
regime in which static schemes are most stressed: STIP \citep{stip} and
related static-permutation designs are secure only while the weights are
private, and collapse once an attacker who knows the public weights $W$ can
solve for the secret transform from the obfuscated $W'$ \citep{belikov_gelo}.

\paragraph{4. Observation surface.}
What the adversary actually sees. \emph{Service I/O}: AloePri's server observes
only obfuscated prompts, obfuscated responses, and the obfuscated weights it
executes. \emph{Accelerator activations}: GELO, ObfuscaTune, and the TEE-split
designs offload mixed hidden states to untrusted VRAM, where the adversary has
full read access. \emph{Storage access patterns}: Caprise \citep{ye_caprise},
RemoteRAG \citep{cheng2025remoterag}, and Opal expose which records are
touched per query. \emph{Network} metadata sits beneath all of these. The
surface is fixed by the architecture---and, as we argue in \cref{sec:eval}, it
is the surface, not the defender's stated intent, that an attack ultimately
exploits.

\paragraph{5. Side-channel scope.}
Whether microarchitectural, cache, timing, power, or physical channels are in
or out of scope. As \cref{tab:threat-taxonomy} shows, every surveyed scheme
places them \emph{out}; \cref{ssec:sidechannel} treats that uniformity as a
finding rather than an accident.

\paragraph{6. Protection goal.}
What must remain hidden: \emph{input data} only; \emph{input and output}
(AloePri protects both prompt and response); the \emph{model weights} as well
(ObfuscaTune and TwinShield additionally shield a proprietary model from the
cloud); or \emph{access patterns} over a corpus (Opal and the RAG-storage
schemes). Two schemes that agree on every other axis can diverge sharply here:
hiding a proprietary model is a different problem from hiding a user's query.

\subsection{The Landscape}
\label{sec:threat-landscape}

\cref{tab:threat-taxonomy} places a representative scheme from each of the five
mechanism families on the six axes. Three patterns stand out. First, the
obfuscation family clusters tightly at \emph{HbC + white-box}: these schemes
explicitly assume the attacker knows the open-source model and the method, and
stake everything on an ephemeral secret. Second, the trust anchor varies more
than the adversary does---schemes that face nearly the same attacker
nonetheless root trust in nothing, a CPU-TEE, or a GPU-TEE, which is the real
axis of disagreement in the field. Third, Opal is alone in defending a
\emph{malicious} host; every other scheme assumes the operator follows the
protocol. The protection goal also splits the table: data-only schemes and
those additionally guarding a proprietary model are solving different
problems, even when their machinery looks similar.

\subsection{The Attacker's Capability Budget}
\label{sec:threat-budget}

The six axes say what an adversary \emph{is}; they do not say how \emph{much}
it can spend. Yet the capability budget---compute, and especially the
observation horizon (a single inference vs.\ many adaptive runs)---is decisive,
because the surveyed schemes claim security against markedly different budgets.
AloePri is explicit that it ``aims to provide adjustable security for
constrained attackers in real-world scenarios, rather than offering ideal-world
security guarantees against worst-case attackers'' \citep{lin_aloepri}; its
protection rests on per-inference noise calibrated to a Rényi-metric DP budget.
GELO's guarantee is narrower still: per-batch non-identifiability under a fresh,
never-reused mixing matrix, so the attacker faces a \emph{single-batch} blind
source separation problem and---by GELO's own analysis---gains nothing across
batches \citep{belikov_gelo}. A worst-case adversary with unbounded compute and
unlimited adaptive observation defeats almost every obfuscation scheme here; an
evaluation that assumed one would therefore measure nothing interesting and
would strawman schemes that never claimed that bar.

We treat the budget as an explicit, per-scheme parameter rather than a hidden
constant. Crucially, the ``what breaks'' result of \cref{sec:aloepri} is
conducted \emph{strictly within AloePri's own constrained-attacker regime}: the
attacker is white-box on weights and method, is denied the secret permutation,
and operates under the same compute and observation horizon AloePri's own
evaluation permits. A break under those conditions is a break by the victim's

\subsection{Finding: the Field-Wide Side-Channel Blind Spot}
\label{ssec:sidechannel}

Reading down the side-channel column of \cref{tab:threat-taxonomy} yields a
uniform answer: \emph{every} scheme places microarchitectural, cache, timing,
power, and physical channels out of scope. GELO excludes ``side channels
originating inside the TEE \dots\ and accelerator-side leakage beyond raw memory
reads'' \citep{belikov_gelo}; Opal devotes a paragraph to enumerating the
in-enclave side channels it does \emph{not} defend \citep{kaviani_opal}; the
remainder simply omit them. This is a collective blind spot worth naming,
because it falls hardest on the schemes least able to absorb it: the TEE and
hybrid designs anchor their entire trust in hardware whose best-documented
weakness is exactly microarchitectural and transient-execution leakage---the
Spectre/Foreshadow-class attacks \needswork{cite Spectre, Foreshadow, PLATYPUS,
and the TEE side-channel surveys}. A scheme that trusts a TEE while declaring
side channels out of scope is assuming away the dominant historical failure
mode of that very anchor.

We do not close this gap: side channels are out of scope for our measurements
as well (\cref{sec:eval}), and current confidential-computing hardware
(TDX-backed CVMs, GPUs in confidential mode) materially raises the bar against
them \citep{kaviani_opal}. But we record it as a systematisation finding and a
standing item for future work: the field has converged on a threat model that
structurally excludes the attack class most likely to break its dominant trust
anchor.

\subsection{Relation to Our Measurement Adversary}
\label{sec:threat-ours}

Our measured comparison (\cref{sec:eval}) instantiates one point in this space:
an honest-but-curious cloud operator, white-box on the public model and the
obfuscation method, denied only the ephemeral secret, observing the untrusted
accelerator and the obfuscated service I/O, with side channels out of scope and
a constrained, realistic budget; its goal is to recover the input prompt.

One subtlety follows directly from the observation-surface axis and shapes how
we read the ``holds vs.\ breaks'' result. AloePri runs the entire obfuscated
model on the server, so its adversary sees obfuscated I/O and weights; a
TEE-split design exposes obfuscated \emph{activations} on the untrusted GPU.
These are different observation surfaces---and that difference is not a
confound but the \emph{independent variable}, because the surface \emph{is} the
defence. Our comparison therefore holds the adversary, the attack goal, the
attack suite, and the base model fixed, and lets the observation surface vary
by architecture. ``Like-for-like'' here means \emph{same adversary, same
target, same attacks}---not identical observed bytes. We make this scope
explicit precisely so that a defence which holds is credited for resisting the
same attacker that breaks another, rather than for being measured on an easier
surface.
